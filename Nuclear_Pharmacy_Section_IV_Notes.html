Intro to QC

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

Why perform RP Quality Control?

  • Assures that RPs are intended to establish their desired biological and radiopharmaceutical characteristics

When are RP Quality Control Tests Performed?

  • After the radionuclide is produced
    • Verifies the radionuclide is suitable to make a radiopharmaceutical
  • After 99mTc is eluted from a generator
    • Verifies that 99mTc is suitable for radiopharmaceutical production
  • After a radiopharmaceutical kit is made
    • Verifies that the RP kit is suitable for patient administration

For each QC test it is important to know:

  1. The nature of the test — what the test is
  2. Why you are performing the QC test
  3. Examples of the QC test
  4. Who performs the QC test
  5. How to perform the QC test
  6. The results (and range of error if applicable)
  7. What could happen if the results are bad

Two Basic Types of QC Tests

  1. Physiochemical — determines the purity and integrity of the RP; tests the physical and chemical properties of radiopharmaceuticals
  2. Biologic — determines the state of the radiopharmaceutical

Physiochemical QC Tests

  1. Physical Properties
  2. pH
  3. Ionic Strength
  4. Radionuclidic Purity
  5. Chemical Purity
  6. Radiochemical Purity

Physical Properties

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

Physical Properties include:

  1. Color and State of the RP
    • You need to know the color and state of your radiopharmaceutical — this makes you aware of any problems
    • Most RPs are clear and should not have any clumps
    • To determine color and state: briefly inspect the RP kit visually
    • If color or state is off: throw the kit away and make a new one
  2. Size and Number of Particles
    • Some RPs have particles — you need to know the size and number
    • 99mTc-MAA (Macroaggregated Albumin)
      • 10–90 µm (no particle larger than 150 µm)
      • A kit contains 1–16 million particles per mg of Aggregated Albumin
      • A standard dose of MAA: 200,000–400,000 particles (minimum 100,000)
    • 99mTc-Sulfur Colloid: 0.1–1 µm
    • The manufacturer tests particle size and count using a Hemocytometer
      • A grid (usually 50 µm blocks) used for counting cells or particles under a microscope
    • If particles are larger than specified: they may lodge in a small artery or vein

pH

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

pH Basics:

  • pH is the acidity of an aqueous solution
  • Expressed numerically with no units; ranges from 0 – 14
pH < 7Solution is Acidic
pH = 7Solution is Neutral
pH > 7Solution is Basic
  • Blood has a pH between 7.35 – 7.45 (slightly basic)
  • Since most RPs are injected IV, we must test the pH of our solution

Two Ways to Measure pH

  1. pH Paper — pH determined by color change; quick, easy, cheap
  2. pH Meter — measures pH electronically; more accurate; more expensive

Ionic Strength & Radionuclidic Purity

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

Ionic Strength

  • The strength of the bond between the pharmaceutical and the radionuclide
  • If ionic strength is low, there is a good chance the bond will break
    • A broken bond leaves Free-99mTc in your kit

Radionuclidic Purity

  • The fraction of total radioactivity present in the form of the desired radionuclide
  • Common examples of radionuclidic impurities:
    1. 99Mo in your 99mTc elution
    2. 124I or 131I in your 123I solution
  • The impurity can be an isotope (same element) or an entirely different radionuclide

Moly Breakthrough Test (Lead Shield Method)

  • Tests for 99Mo in 99mTc elution — performed by a Nuc Med Tech on every elution
  1. Place the 99mTc elution in a Lead Pig (thick enough to stop all 99mTc photons; only 99Mo photons escape)
  2. Place the Lead Pig in a Moly Breakthrough Tester
  3. Place tester in a dose calibrator set to 99Mo
  4. Measure the amount of 99Mo (result in µCi)
  5. Divide µCi of 99Mo by mCi of 99mTc in the elution

Limit: 0.15 µCi of 99Mo per mCi of 99mTc at time of administration

If above the limit:

  1. Throw out the elution
  2. Elute the generator again
  3. Test for Moly Breakthrough again
  4. If still abnormal, the generator is probably malfunctioning

Effects of excess 99Mo:

  1. Lower target-to-nontarget ratio (less 99mTc available for the kit)
  2. Increased radiation exposure (99Mo is a beta emitter)

Other fission-produced radionuclidic impurities (I-131, Zr-99, Te-132, Ru-103, Sb-124, Cs-134, Rb-86): limit is 0.5 µCi of impurity per mCi of 99mTc; tested at the manufacturer.

Chemical Purity

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5
  • Fraction of the material in the desired chemical form
  • For this QC test we are only concerned with non-radioactive chemicals that could cause impurities
  • At this point, the only chemical of concern is alumina from the generator column — this is called Alumina Breakthrough

Testing for Alumina Breakthrough

  1. Obtain an alumina strip (usually pink in color)
  2. Place a drop of a standard (representing 10 µg of alumina) on one side of the strip
  3. Place a drop of the 99mTc-Eluate on the other side of the strip

Limit: 10 µg of Alumina per mL of 99mTc-Eluate

This is a colorimetric technique — compare the colors of the standard vs. the eluate drop.

  • If eluate drop is lighter than the standard: within limits ✓
  • If eluate drop is darker than the standard: outside limits ✗

Performed by: a Nuc Med Tech on every elution of Na99mTcO4

If outside normal limits:

  • Repeat the test to rule out errors
  • If still abnormal, elute the generator again
  • If a second elution is still abnormal, there is a problem with the generator

Radiochemical Purity

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5
  • Fraction of the total radioactivity in the desired chemical form
  • The RP kit is made up; we know we have the correct radionuclide (99mTc)
  • We want to check if 99mTc is in the correct chemical form: Bound-99mTc
  • Examples of radiochemical impurities: Free-99mTc, Hydrolyzed-99mTc
  • Tested by the manufacturer

Methods of Testing Radiochemical Purity

  1. Precipitation — A reagent is added; the radiochemical is separated by centrifuge; impurity is counted.
  2. Paper & ITLC (Chromatography) — Drop of elution placed on bottom of strip; strip suspended in solvent; different chemicals migrate to different levels; strips are cut and counted to obtain Free-to-Bound ratio. Most common technique.
  3. Gel Chromatography — Separates by molecular size; larger molecules migrate faster; Bound-99mTc fastest, then Free-99mTc, Hydrolyzed-99mTc remains on column.
  4. Gel & Paper Electrophoresis — Separates by chemical charge; voltage applied to sample; counted after separation.
  5. Ion Exchange — Sample passed through ionic resin; certain radiochemicals stick and can be counted.
  6. Solvent Extraction — Combined with immiscible liquid; radiochemicals separate by solubility.
  7. HPLC (High Pressure Liquid Chromatography) — Like liquid chromatography but with larger columns and high-pressure pumps; fastest and most common method for testing radiochemical impurities.
  8. Distillation — Separates by vapor pressure; different radiochemicals distill off at different temperatures.

Causes of Radiochemical Impurity

  • Change in temperature or pH
  • Light exposure
  • Oxidizing and reducing agents
  • Radiolysis

Effects of Radiochemical Impurity

  • Increased radiation exposure to patient
  • Decreased target-to-nontarget ratio

Biologic Tests & Sterility

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

Biologic QC Tests examine:

  1. Sterility
  2. Pyrogenicity
  3. Toxicity
  • The FDA requires that all RPs be sterile and pyrogen free due to IV administration
  • These tests are performed by the manufacturer

Sterility

Performed to ensure radiopharmaceuticals are free of viable microorganisms. Sterility procedures use aseptic techniques — sterile products are used throughout, so no bacteria is added during the procedure.

Two Methods of Sterilization

  1. Autoclaving — Steam at 121°C for 15–20 minutes; kills microorganisms; not suitable for heat-labile or short-lived RPs; commonly used for glassware and heat-stable solid products.
  2. Filtration (most common) — Membrane filter with pores small enough to trap microorganisms; 0.45 µm pores most common; 0.22 µm for blood products; works for heat-labile and short-lived RPs.

Three Tests to Verify Sterility

  1. Fluid Thioglycolate Medium (FTM)
    • Incubate sample equal to a normal RP dose at 30°C for 14 days
    • If bacterial growth is present after 14 days: microorganisms are present → RP is asterile
  2. Soybean Casein Digest (SCD)
    • Incubate sample at 20°C for 14 days
    • If bacterial growth is present: RP is asterile
  3. 14C Glucose Test
    • Incubate sample for 3–24 hours (no specific temperature)
    • Microorganisms present if 14CO2 is detected — microorganisms metabolize glucose and produce CO2, which combines with radioactive 14C
    • 14CO2 is counted to determine presence of microorganisms

Pyrogenicity

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5
  • All RPs must be pyrogen free before human administration
  • Pyrogens result from metabolic byproducts (endotoxins) of microorganisms

Problems with Pyrogens

  • Very small (0.05–1 µm) — filters that small would also filter the RP
  • Fairly heat stable — autoclaving is not always effective
  • Cause fever, chills, and sweating — usually not fatal but must be prevented

Prevention

  • Use sterile glassware, solutions, and equipment
  • Use aseptic techniques when preparing RPs
  • To eliminate pyrogens if present: dry heat at 175°C for several hours

Two Tests for Pyrogens

  1. Limulus Amebocyte Lysate Test (LAL)
    • Tests for endotoxin-type pyrogens
    • Uses lysates of amebocytes from the blood of the horseshoe crab
    • Mix the lysate with your RP; incubate 15–60 minutes at 37°C
    • Pyrogens are present if an opaque gel forms — thicker gel = more pyrogens
  2. USP Rabbit Test
    • Based on a febrile response
    • 3 rabbits are injected with the RP
    • Temperature taken after 3 hours — an increase (febrile response) indicates pyrogens

Toxicity

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5
  • Before any RP is used for humans, the toxic effect and safe dosage must be established
  • Toxic effects are usually from the pharmaceutical — not the radionuclide

Two Ways to Test Toxicity

  1. Animal Testing — Animals are injected and studied for their responses
  2. Computer Models and Cell Culture — Simulated effects; preferred method due to difficulty in animal testing

LD50/60

  • The lethal dose required to produce 50% mortality in 60 days
  • Establishes the safe dose for humans

RP Kits — Chemical Bonds

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

RP Kits overview — topics covered:

  1. Chemical Bonds
  2. Methods of Radiolabeling
  3. Factors in Labeling
  4. Contents of an RP Kit
  5. Proper method of preparing a RP Kit

Four Types of Chemical Bonds

  1. Ionic Bonds
    • Like charges repel; unlike charges attract
    • A positive and negative ion are attracted to each other and bond (e.g., Na+ attracts Cl-)
  2. Covalent Bonds
    • Two atoms share their outer shell electrons; each atom donates electrons
    • An atom with 8 outer shell electrons is more stable
    • Example: two fluorine atoms each donate one electron → both now have 8 outer shell electrons
  3. Coordinate Covalent Bonds
    • Two atoms bond via outer shell electrons — similar to a covalent bond
    • However, only one atom (the donor atom) donates electrons
    • Example: O in H2O donates 2 electrons to bond with Cu
  4. Chelation
    • Multiple coordinate covalent bonds are formed
    • The donor atom donates more than one pair of electrons
    • Example: DTPA forms chelation bonds with 99mTc

RP Kits — Radiolabeling Methods

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

Four Main Methods of Radiolabeling

  1. Isotope Exchange Reaction
    • One or more atoms in a molecule are replaced by an isotope of the same element (same Z#, different A#)
    • Same biologic and chemical properties as the original molecule
    • Example: 125I-Labeled T3 — start with non-radioactive T3 (has stable iodine); add 125I; result is 125I-Labeled T3
  2. Introduction of a Foreign Label
    • A radionuclide is added to a molecule with a known biologic role
    • Bonds formed via covalent, coordinate covalent, or chelation
    • Examples: 99mTc-Labeled RPs; 111In-Labeled WBCs
    • In 99mTc-DTPA: DTPA bonds to 99mTc by chelation
  3. Labeling with Bifunctional Chelating Agents
    • Two chelating bonds formed: macromolecule (protein/antibody) on one side; radionuclide on the other
    • Example: 99mTc-DTPA-Antibody — 99mTc on one side, DTPA in the middle, antibody on the other side
  4. Biosynthesis
    • A living organism is grown in a culture medium containing the radioactive tracer
    • Example: 57Co-Cyanocobalamin (Vitamin B12) — Cyanocobalamin incubated with 57Co; they grow together to form the labeled compound

RP Kits — Factors in Labeling

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

Eight Factors in Labeling

  1. Efficiency — The amount of pharmaceuticals labeled to radionuclides; expressed as a percentage; higher is better
  2. Stability — The bond between the radionuclide and the pharmaceutical should be strong (stable); covalent and coordinate covalent bonds are strong
  3. Denaturation — The structure or biologic properties of the RP are altered; heating may alter properties
  4. Isotope Effect — Occurs when isotopes are present in a radiopharmaceutical (e.g., isotope exchange reaction); different isotope weights may change physical properties
  5. Storage Conditions
    • Proteins can be denatured by heat → store in refrigerator (e.g., 99mTc-MAA)
    • Light can break down some compounds → store in the dark
  6. Radiolysis — Labeled compounds can be broken down by radiation from the radionuclide (e.g., 99mTc radiation may break the bond in 99mTc-HDP)
  7. Shelf Life
    • Factors affecting shelf life: T1/2, radiolysis, additives, solvent, chemical bonds
    • Maximum shelf life: 3 physical T1/2 or 6 months
    • 99mTc-RPs: maximum shelf life of 18 hours; range 0.5–18 hours; 6 hours is most common
    • Minimum shelf life: 1.5× the length of the study (e.g., 6 hours minimum for a 4-hour bone scan)
  8. Purification — Radionuclides will have some impurities; it is important to remove or minimize them

Contents of an RP Kit

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5

The Lyophilized (Freeze-Dried) Mixture

  • Everything is contained in a lyophilized mixture
    • Lyophilization preserves the mixture and allows it to dissolve in Na99mTcO4 aqueous solution
    • Appears as a white disc sitting at the bottom of the kit vial

Contents of the Lyophilized Disc

  • 1 mg of the Pharmaceutical (DTPA, HDP, etc.)
  • 0.5 mg of Reducing Agent (Stannous Chloride)
  • 0.5 mg of Additives and Preservatives
  • pH Adjuster (Sodium Hydroxide/NaOH or HCl)

Additives and Preservatives

  • Function as stabilizers: maintain integrity, keep the RP in its original state (ascorbic acid, gentisic acid, citrates, acetates)
  • Act as bactericidal agents: prevent bacterial growth (benzyl alcohol — also prevents radiolysis)
  • Must not react with other ingredients of the RP kit

Proper Method for Preparing an RP Kit

  1. Use ASEPTIC techniques
  2. Swab the RP kit vial with alcohol
  3. Inject the appropriate amount of radionuclide (each kit has specific activity and volume limits; e.g., 20–100 mCi in 1–6 mL)
  4. Mix the pharmaceutical (each kit has its own method: invert, swirl, or water bath)
  5. Assay the vial and complete the label for the kit
  6. Incubate the vial if required

Glossary

📘 NM&MI Tech · Ch. 5
Quality Control (QC)
Testing to assure that radiopharmaceuticals have their desired biological and radiopharmaceutical characteristics before patient administration.
Physiochemical QC
Tests that determine the purity and integrity of the RP by examining physical and chemical properties. Includes physical properties, pH, ionic strength, radionuclidic purity, chemical purity, and radiochemical purity.
Biologic QC
Tests that examine sterility, pyrogenicity, and toxicity of RPs before human administration. Required by the FDA for all IV-administered RPs.
Hemocytometer
A grid used to count cells or particles under a microscope. Used to verify the size and number of particles in RPs such as 99mTc-MAA.
Radionuclidic Purity
The fraction of total radioactivity present in the form of the desired radionuclide. Tested to ensure there are no unwanted radionuclidic impurities (e.g., 99Mo in 99mTc).
Moly Breakthrough
The presence of 99Mo in a 99mTc elution. Tested using the Lead Shield Method. Limit: 0.15 µCi of 99Mo per mCi of 99mTc at time of administration.
Chemical Purity
Fraction of material in the desired chemical form, specifically regarding non-radioactive chemical impurities. Alumina breakthrough is the primary concern for 99mTc elutions. Limit: 10 µg/mL.
Alumina Breakthrough
Contamination of the 99mTc elution with alumina from the generator column. Tested by colorimetric comparison with a 10 µg/mL standard on an alumina strip.
Radiochemical Purity
Fraction of total radioactivity in the desired chemical form. Impurities include Free-99mTc and Hydrolyzed-99mTc. Most commonly tested by ITLC or HPLC.
ITLC
Instant Thin Layer Chromatography — a paper/strip-based chromatographic method for separating and quantifying radiochemical species. Free-99mTc and Bound-99mTc migrate to different levels on the strip.
HPLC
High Pressure Liquid Chromatography — separates radiochemical species using high-pressure pumps; fastest and most common method for testing radiochemical impurities.
Sterility
The state of being free from viable microorganisms. RPs must be sterile before human administration. Common method: filtration through 0.45 µm (or 0.22 µm) membrane.
Aseptic Technique
Procedures performed using sterile products throughout, preventing introduction of microorganisms during RP preparation.
Pyrogen
A substance (endotoxin from microbial metabolic byproducts) that causes fever, chills, and sweating when injected. Pyrogens are heat stable, very small (0.05–1 µm), and cannot be filtered out.
LAL Test
Limulus Amebocyte Lysate Test — uses lysate from horseshoe crab blood to detect endotoxin pyrogens. Positive if an opaque gel forms after 15–60 min incubation at 37°C.
USP Rabbit Test
Pyrogen test in which 3 rabbits are injected with the RP; an increase in body temperature after 3 hours indicates pyrogens.
LD50/60
Lethal Dose causing 50% mortality in 60 days. Used to establish the safe dose of an RP for human administration.
Lyophilization
Freeze-drying; the process used to preserve the contents of an RP kit as a white disc that dissolves when Na99mTcO4 is added.
Stannous Chloride (SnCl₂)
The reducing agent (0.5 mg) included in RP kits to reduce 99mTc from 7+ to a lower oxidation state, enabling it to bond with the pharmaceutical.
Chelation
A type of chemical bonding where a donor atom forms multiple coordinate covalent bonds. Used by DTPA to bind 99mTc in kits like 99mTc-DTPA.
Radiolysis
Breakdown of labeled compounds by radiation emitted by the radionuclide itself. A key factor limiting shelf life of RPs.
Shelf Life
The length of time a labeled RP remains usable. Maximum: 3 physical T1/2 or 6 months. Minimum: 1.5× the length of the study. 99mTc-RPs: 0.5–18 hours (most commonly 6 hours).
Biosynthesis (Radiolabeling)
A radiolabeling method in which a living organism is grown in a medium containing the radioactive tracer. Example: 57Co-Cyanocobalamin (Vit B12).
Isotope Exchange Reaction
A radiolabeling method in which one or more atoms in a molecule are replaced by a radioactive isotope of the same element. Example: 125I-Labeled T3.
