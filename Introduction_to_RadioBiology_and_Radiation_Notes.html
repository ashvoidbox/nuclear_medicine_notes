Radiation

What do we know about radiation:

  • Radiation is classified into two main categories:
    • Non-ionizing radiation (cannot ionize matter)
    • Ionizing radiation (can ionize matter)
  • Non-Ionizing Radiation (NIR)
    • Does not have enough energy to remove electrons from atoms and molecules
    • 2 main categories
      • Optical Radiation - Visible and Infared
      • Electromagnetic field - Power frequencies, microwaves, and radio frequencies
        • Longer wavelengths and shorter frequency
    • What are the risks of NIR
      • Depends on energy and exposure time
        • Increase each and the effects increase
        • Generally just causes localized heating of the area
        • A burn can occur if exposure time is increased
  • Ionizing radiation contains two major categories
    • Directly ionizing radiation (charged particles).
      • Electrons, protons, alpha particles, heavy ions.
        • Particulate Radiation
        • They carry a charge
          • This allows them to directly intereact with atomic electrons and can remove them
    • Indirectly ionizing radiation (neutral particles).
      • Photons (x rays, gamma rays)
        • Electromagnetic Radiation
          • They are neutral and do not interact with atomic electrons
      • Except neurtrons are actually particulate but indirectly ionizing radiation
        • It is indirect because it does not carry a charge
  • Did you think I would forget about Ultraviolet (UV) radiation
    • Low energy UV is generally NIR
    • Medium to High UV is ionizing
      • Yes; increased exposure will cause a sunburn

Gamma

Gamma Radiation (γ)

  • Is not a particle
  • Size:
    • It is small
    • Smallest form of radiation

    • Can measure personal exposure

  • SI Units

Absorbed Dose

Absorbed Dose

  • How much energy is being transferred from the radiation to the absorbing material
    • Absorbing material could be the patient
    • T-Units - rad - Radiation Absorbed Dose
    • SI Units - Gray (Gy)
      • One Gy = 100 rads
  • The Fraction of radiation Absorbed is the f-factor
    • The f-factor depends on:
      • Energy of the radiation
      • Penetrability of the radiation
      • Composition of the absorbing material
  • Absorbed dose doesn't take into account the type of radiation
  • Radiation has a Quality Factor
    • Factor used in radiation to weigh the absorbed dose with regard to its presumed biological effectiveness
      • X-rays and Gamma rays are 1
      • Beta = 1
      • Alpha = 20

 

13

Dose Equivalent

Dose Equivalent

  • The absorbed dose times a quality factor:
    • This takes into account the type of radiation
    • This is used for occupational exposure
    • T-Units - rem - Radiation Equivalent Man
    • SI Units - Seivert (Sv)
      • 1 Sv = 100 rem

Radioactivity

Radioactivity

  • Radioactivity is a measure of:
    • Radiation Intensity
    • Radioactive decay of a material
  • The activity of a radioisotope is simply a measure of how many atoms undergo radioactive decay per a unit of time
  • T-Units:
    • Curie (Ci)
      • Quantity of any radioactive nuclide in which the number of disintegrations per second is 3.7 X 1010
  • SI Units:
    • Becquerel (Bq)
      • Quantity of any radioactive nuclide in which the rate of decay equals 1 dps

Half-Life

Half-Life (T1/2)

  • Radioactivity decreases over time
  • The time it takes for a radioactive material to decay to 50% of it's original activity
  • A common half-life is 99mTc which has a T1/2 of 6 hours
    • It takes 6 hours for 100 mCi of 99mTc to decay to 50 mCi

Radon and Radon Gas

  • Radon is found in nearly all rocks and soils
    • Radium disintegrates into alpha particles and atoms of radon
      • 10 to 50% of the radon atoms escape from the mineral grain into the underground "soil gas"
        • Gas can be taken straight up or follow long underground pockets

 

  • Radon is an invisible and odorless radioactive gas
    • Radon is the heaviest known gas
      • Nine times heavier than air
    • Radon is an aberration
      • The only gas in the long decay chain of heavy metal elements
  • Radon gas is produced by the natural disintegration of radioactive heavy metals
    • Uranium and thorium
      • Which are dispersed throughout the earth's crust
      • We will never run out of uranium or thorium
      • The radioactive half-life of Uranium-238 is 4.5 billion years
      • The half=life of Thorium-232 is 14.1 billion years
      • Their decay chain continuously produces radium, which decays into radon isotopes
        • Radon-222 (common in homes)
        • Radon-220 (Thoron)
      • In each step of the radioactive decay chain the atom nuclei emits radiation
        • Alpha, beta and gamma
  • Radon is found in nearly all rocks and soils
    • Radium disintegrates into alpha particles and atoms of radon
      • 10 to 50% of the radon atoms escape from the mineral grain into the underground "soil gas"
        • Gas can be taken straight up or follow long underground pockets

        • They produce Radon-222 and Radon-220 (Thoron)

Glossary of Terms

RadioBiology
The study of the action and effects of ionizing radiation on living things, especially the health effects of radiation.
Ionizing Radiation
Radiation with enough energy to remove electrons from atoms and molecules, creating ion pairs. Includes alpha, beta, gamma, x-rays, and neutrons.
Non-Ionizing Radiation (NIR)
Radiation that does not have enough energy to ionize atoms. Includes radio waves, microwaves, visible light, and infrared. Generally causes localized heating.
Directly Ionizing Radiation
Charged particles (electrons, protons, alpha particles, heavy ions) that interact directly with atomic electrons through their electric charge.
Indirectly Ionizing Radiation
Neutral particles (photons, neutrons) that do not carry a charge and ionize indirectly by setting charged particles in motion.
Alpha Particle (α)
A helium ion consisting of 2 protons and 2 neutrons; charge +2. The largest, slowest, and least penetrating form of radiation — stopped by paper.
Beta Particle (β−)
A high-speed electron emitted from the nucleus; charge −1. Moderately penetrating — travels a few cm in tissue and a few meters in air.
Positron (β+)
The antimatter counterpart of a beta particle; charge +1. Undergoes annihilation with an electron to produce two 511 keV photons.
Annihilation
The interaction of a positron and an electron in which both are destroyed and two 511 keV photons are emitted in opposite directions.
Gamma Ray (γ)
Electromagnetic radiation emitted from an excited nucleus; no charge or mass. The smallest, fastest, and most penetrating form — requires lead to stop it.
X-ray
Electromagnetic radiation originating outside the nucleus (from electron transitions or Bremsstrahlung); same properties as gamma rays but different origin.
Exposure
A measure of the amount of ionization produced in air by x or gamma radiation. Traditional unit: Roentgen (R). SI unit: C/kg. 1 R = 2.58 × 10−4 C/kg.
Roentgen (R)
Traditional unit of exposure. 1 R = 2.58 × 10−4 coulombs per kilogram of air.
Absorbed Dose
The energy deposited by radiation per unit mass of absorbing material. Traditional unit: rad. SI unit: Gray (Gy). 1 Gy = 100 rad.
rad
Radiation Absorbed Dose — traditional unit of absorbed dose. 1 rad = 0.01 J/kg of energy deposited.
Gray (Gy)
SI unit of absorbed dose. 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad.
Quality Factor (QF)
A dimensionless factor that weights absorbed dose by the biological effectiveness of the radiation type. X/gamma/beta = 1; alpha = 20.
f-Factor
The fraction of radiation absorbed by a material. Depends on the energy and penetrability of the radiation and the composition of the absorbing material.
Dose Equivalent
Absorbed dose multiplied by the quality factor; accounts for the type of radiation. Used for occupational exposure. Traditional unit: rem. SI unit: Sievert (Sv).
rem
Radiation Equivalent Man — traditional unit of dose equivalent. rem = rad × Quality Factor.
Sievert (Sv)
SI unit of dose equivalent. 1 Sv = 100 rem.
Radioactivity
A measure of the rate of radioactive decay of a material — how many atoms undergo decay per unit time. Traditional unit: Curie (Ci). SI unit: Becquerel (Bq).
Curie (Ci)
Traditional unit of radioactivity. 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
Becquerel (Bq)
SI unit of radioactivity. 1 Bq = 1 disintegration per second.
Half-Life (T½)
The time required for a radioactive material to decay to 50% of its original activity. 99mTc has a T½ of 6 hours.
Natural Background Radiation
Radiation from natural sources including cosmic radiation, terrestrial radiation, radon gas, and internal radioactive elements in the body.
Radon (Rn-222)
A naturally occurring, invisible, odorless radioactive gas produced by the decay of uranium and thorium. The heaviest known gas; primary indoor radiation source.
Cosmic Radiation
Radiation originating from the sun and stars that bombards the earth. Increases with altitude and latitude toward the poles. ~35 mrad/yr at sea level; ~300 mrad/yr for jet pilots.
Terrestrial Radiation
Radiation from naturally occurring radioactive materials in the earth's crust — uranium, thorium, radium, and potassium-40. ~50 mrad/yr.
Man-made Radiation
Radiation from artificial sources including medical x-rays (~39 mrem/yr), nuclear medicine (~14 mrem/yr), nuclear power plants, and consumer products.
Traditional (T) Units
The conventional radiation unit system common in the US: Roentgen (R), rad, rem, and Curie (Ci).
SI Units
Système International radiation units common outside the US: C/kg, Gray (Gy), Sievert (Sv), and Becquerel (Bq).
