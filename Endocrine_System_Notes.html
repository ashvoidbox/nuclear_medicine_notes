Endocrine Glands

Types of Glands

The Endocrine System has 2 types of glands:

  • 1. Exocrine glands — do NOT produce hormones
    • Secrete into ducts and taken to organs of the body
    • Produce sudoriferous (sweat), sebaceous (oil), mucous, and digestive juices
  • 2. Endocrine glands — Produce Hormones
    • Secrete their hormones which are picked up by blood and transported throughout the body
    • Endocrine glands include: Hypophysis (Pituitary), Thyroid, Parathyroids, & Adrenals
    • Some organs contain endocrine tissue and produce hormones but are not endocrine glands:
      • Hypothalamus, thymus, pancreas, ovaries, testes, kidneys, stomach, liver, small intestines, skin, heart, and placenta

Puberty and the Endocrine System

  • At puberty (around age 12), the endocrine system really kicks in
  • Sex hormones increase dramatically
  • Girls — fatty tissue increases; Boys — muscle mass increases, longer vocal cords
  • The Endocrine System produces hormones that drive these changes
4

Hormones

What Are Hormones?

  • Hormones — "Chemicals having a specific effect on the activity of a specific organ"
  • Hormones are messengers released from the endocrine system to tell organs what to do
  • Hormones are stimulated and inhibited by:
    • 1. Nervous System
    • 2. Chemical changes in blood
    • 3. Other hormones
  • If hormones are not produced naturally, they can be administered:
    • 1. By mouth — thyroid hormones (can survive digestion)
    • 2. By injection into the bloodstream — insulin (would be broken down by digestion)

Target Cells

  • Although hormones travel throughout the body, each hormone affects only specific cells called target cells
  • Target cells have receptors specific to a hormone
  • Receptors can be:
    • 1. On the outside of the cells (Extracellular)
    • 2. On the inside of the cells (Intracellular)

Effects of Hormones

  • 1. Regulate body fluid
  • 2. Regulate metabolism and energy balance
  • 3. Regulate contraction of muscles
  • 4. Regulate the immune system
  • 5. Help in growth and development
  • 6. Help in sperm and egg production
5

Hypothalamus

  • The hypothalamus is located in the inferior central portion of the brain
  • Considered the "master" gland
  • The hypothalamus controls the Pituitary gland
    • The Pituitary used to be called the master gland
    • The Pituitary secretes several hormones that control other endocrine glands
  • The hypothalamus is the link between the nervous system and the endocrine system
  • It receives input from the brain to secrete hormones

Functions of the Hypothalamus

  • Overall, the hypothalamus secretes 9 hormones that regulate:
    • Body temperature, thirst, hunger, sexual behavior, and defensive reactions such as fear and rage
  • The Hypothalamus & Pituitary together regulate virtually all aspects of growth, development, metabolism, and homeostasis
6

Pituitary Gland

  • Also called the Hypophysis
  • A pea-sized gland attached just below the Hypothalamus
  • Has 2 anatomically and functionally separate portions:
    • 1. Anterior Pituitary
    • 2. Posterior Pituitary

Anterior Pituitary — 7 Major Hormones

  • Release and inhibition depend on the hypothalamus
  • Secretes:
    • 1. Human Growth Hormone (hGH) — stimulates body growth and regulates metabolism
    • 2. Prolactin (PRL) — initiates milk production in mammary glands
    • 3. Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH) — stimulates adrenal cortex to produce glucocorticoids
    • 4. Melanocyte-Stimulating Hormone (MSH) — affects skin pigmentation
    • 5. Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH) — controls thyroid gland secretions
    • 6. Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) — secretion of estrogen & production of sperm
    • 7. Luteinizing Hormone (LH) — stimulates sexual and reproductive activities

Posterior Pituitary — 2 Stored/Secreted Hormones

  • Does not synthesize hormones; stores and secretes:
    • 1. Oxytocin (OT) — enhances contractions during childbirth; stimulates milk ejection after birth
    • 2. Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH) — decreases urine production

Anterior Pituitary Defects

  • 1. Pituitary Dwarfism — hyposecretion of hGH during childhood; slow bone growth; epiphyseal plates close prematurely
  • 2. Giantism — hypersecretion of hGH during childhood; increased long bones; very tall stature ("Andre the Giant" had this)
  • 3. Acromegaly — hypersecretion of hGH during adulthood; bones thicken (hands, feet, cheeks, jaw) since they can no longer get longer

Posterior Pituitary Defect

  • 1. Diabetes Insipidus — hyposecretion of ADH; excretion of large amounts of urine; dehydration is the main concern

Thyroid Gland

Anatomy

  • Located just below the Larynx (voice box)
  • Has right & left lobes on either side of the trachea, connected by an Isthmus
  • Weighs about 30 g (1 oz) and is 3–4 cm long normally
  • Very rich blood supply
  • When born, the thyroid sits at the base of the tongue and migrates down — if it remains there it is called a Lingual Thyroid

Thyroid Follicles

  • Most of the thyroid is filled with microscopic spherical sacs called thyroid follicles, containing:
    • 1. Follicular cells — manufacture thyroid hormones (T3 & T4)
    • 2. Parafollicular cells — produce calcitonin
  • The thyroid is the only endocrine gland that stores its product in large quantities (normally ~100-day supply)

8 Steps of Thyroid Hormone Production

  • 1. Follicular cells trap Iodide (I−)
  • 2. Follicular cells make thyroglobulin (TGB) — a protein of 5000 amino acids including tyrosine
  • 3. Oxidation of Iodide: 2I− → I2
  • 4. Iodination of Tyrosine:
    • 1 Iodine bound = Monoiodotyrosine (T1)
    • 2 Iodine bound = Diiodotyrosine (T2)
  • 5. Coupling: T2 + T2 = T4 (Thyroxine) | T1 + T2 = T3 (Triiodothyronine)
  • 6. Separation of hormone: T3 & T4 separated from tyrosine; leftover T1 & T2 undergo deiodination and are reused
  • 7. Secretion: T3 & T4 are lipid-soluble and enter the bloodstream
  • 8. Transport: T3 & T4 combine with proteins in the blood for transport

T4 is secreted in greater quantities; T3 is more potent.

Negative Feedback System — Release of Thyroid Hormones

  • 1. High iodine in the thyroid → decreases hormone secretion
  • 2. When thyroid hormones are low:
    • Hypothalamus releases TRH (Thyrotropin Releasing Hormone)
    • TRH stimulates Pituitary to secrete TSH
    • TSH stimulates thyroid to produce and release T3/T4
  • Analogy: Thyroid = furnace | Pituitary = thermostat | Hormones = heat | Hypothalamus = operator

Actions of Thyroid Hormones

  • 1. Regulate metabolism
  • 2. Affect calorie burning, body temperature, and weight
  • 3. Cause heart to beat faster & harder
  • 4. All cells increase their activity rate under the influence of thyroid hormones

Thyroid Disorders

  • 1. Cretinism — hyposecretion during fetal life; skeleton fails to grow and mature
  • 2. Myxedema — hyposecretion during adult years; edema of face, slow heart rate, low body temp, dry hair/skin, muscular weakness
  • 3. Graves Disease — form of hyperthyroidism; antibody mimics TSH; also called Basedow's or Parry's disease
  • 4. Hyperthyroidism — overactive thyroid; too much T3/T4; S&S: palpitations, heat intolerance, nervousness, insomnia, weight loss, trembling, enlarged thyroid
  • 5. Hashimoto's Disease — antibodies bind TSH receptor but stimulate only thyroid growth; hormone secretion not increased
  • 6. Goiter — enlarged thyroid gland; symptom of many thyroid diseases
  • 7. Thyroid Nodules — 50% of population will have one; can be palpated or scanned; hot or cold; solitary or multiple
  • 8. Thyroid Cancer — 5% of nodules are malignant; a cold spot is more likely malignant
8

Parathyroid Gland

  • Small round masses of tissue attached to the thyroid
  • The parathyroid has no related function to the thyroid
  • Secretes Parathyroid Hormone (PTH or parathormone)

Parathyroid Diseases

  • 1. Hyperparathyroidism — too much PTH
    • A tumor in the parathyroid is the main cause
    • Causes high levels of Ca²⁺ which destroys tissues of the body
  • 2. Tetany — form of hypoparathyroidism
    • Causes low levels of Ca²⁺ in the blood
    • Nerves become active without stimulus
    • Muscles will twitch
9

Adrenal Gland

  • The Adrenal Gland lies superior to each kidney
  • Has an outer Adrenal Cortex (AC) and inner Adrenal Medulla

Adrenal Cortex

  • Produces steroid hormones — loss of these = death within 3–7 days
  • A specific steroid produced by the AC is aldosterone:
    • Causes kidneys to increase absorption of Na⁺ and increase excretion of K⁺ & H⁺
    • Removes acid from the body and prevents acidosis
    • Increases absorption of water

Adrenal Gland Diseases

  • 1. Addison's Disease — hyposecretion of aldosterone
    • Failure of the AC to produce and secrete aldosterone
    • Elevated K⁺ and decreased Na⁺
    • Causes: anorexia, nausea & vomiting, weight loss, muscular weakness
  • 2. Cushing's Syndrome — hypersecretion of aldosterone
    • S&S: obesity, hypertension, hair growth

Introduction to Endocrine Radiopharmaceuticals

The radiopharmaceuticals used in the Endocrine System are:

  • 1. The Radioactive Iodines
    • 131I
    • 123I
  • 2. 99mTc — Technetium-99m

Why Radioactive Iodine?

  • Iodine is used in the natural process of making thyroid hormones (T3 & T4)
  • Therefore, radioactive or not, iodine naturally goes to the thyroid
  • This makes radioactive iodine ideal for nuclear medicine imaging of the thyroid
11

Iodine-131 (131I)

Physical Properties

  • Decays by Beta emission
  • Half-life: 8 days
  • Principal gamma energy: 364 keV

Clinical Implications

  • Beta decay (particle emission) is more damaging to tissue
  • Higher than ideal gamma energy for imaging
  • Only 30% efficient for a ½-inch crystal
12

Technetium-99m (99mTc)

  • 99mTc energy: 140 keV, T½: 6 hours
  • 99mTc naturally goes to the thyroid and gets trapped
  • However, it does NOT go through the process of making thyroid hormones
  • This allows it to be used for imaging but NOT for an uptake

Advantages of 99mTc

  • 1. Low radiation dose to patient
  • 2. Higher doses can be administered
  • 3. Image sooner
  • 4. Readily available
  • 5. Ideal energy for imaging

Disadvantages of 99mTc

  • 1. Cannot perform a thyroid uptake

Thyroid Clinical Applications

Clinical Uptake Considerations

Elevated Radioiodine Uptake can indicate:

  • 1. Graves Disease
  • 2. Toxic Nodular Goiter (Plummer's Disease)
  • 3. Tumor Secreting Stimulators

Reduced Radioiodine Uptake can indicate:

  • 1. Failure of gland to respond to TSH
  • 2. Insufficient Pituitary secretion of TSH
  • 3. Thyroiditis
  • 4. Iodine overload (possibly from radiographic contrast)
15

Types of Thyroid Defects

  • 1. Nodules — Lumps in the thyroid
    • May or may not be palpable
    • HOT nodule — Functioning nodule; usually a hyperfunctioning adenoma
    • COLD nodule — Non-functioning nodule; usually a cyst or adenoma; 20% chance of being cancerous
    • Multinodular — has multiple cold and/or hot nodules; thyroid is usually enlarged (goiter)
  • 2. Ectopic Thyroid Tissue — thyroid tissue outside the normal thyroid area
    • Can be in the neck, base of the tongue, pelvis, chest
    • Deep tissue should be imaged with 131I (higher energy to avoid attenuation; longer T½ for delayed images)
  • 3. Thyroiditis — Inflammation of the thyroid; usually shows as coarse patchy distribution
  • 4. Diffuse Toxic Goiter (Graves Disease) — uniform increased activity; usually enlarged; often shows pyramidal lobe; uptake values essential (usually 40–70% range)
  • 5. Thyroid Carcinoma — malignant disease; shows as nodule (hot or cold); can appear as extension of a lobe

Iodine Uptake Test

Purpose

  • Purpose: to TEST FOR THYROID FUNCTION
  • The amount of iodine that goes to the thyroid is related to how the thyroid is functioning
  • Higher uptake = more active thyroid
  • Can use either 123I or 131I

Indications

  • 1. Determine function of the thyroid (Normal / Hypothyroidism / Hyperthyroidism)
  • 2. Distinguish between Graves Disease and Thyroiditis

Patient Prep

  • 1. NPO after midnight — ensures proper digestion and uptake
  • 2. Off thyroid medications — meds could interfere with iodine uptake
  • 3. Optional: low iodine diet for a few days prior

Radiopharmaceutical and Dose

  • 123I — 0.2–0.5 mCi (capsule or liquid)
  • 131I — 0.01 mCi (capsule or liquid)

Thyroid Uptake Procedure

  • 1. Patient arrives 8:00–9:00 am
  • 2. Administer radiopharmaceutical; patient may leave, returns in 4–6 hours
  • 3. Standard counted in a neck phantom with an uptake probe
    • Standard has same activity as patient capsule
    • Neck phantom mimics neck/thyroid for counting
    • CPM obtained for standard
  • 4. Room background taken with uptake probe in appropriate setting
  • 5. Patient returns (4–6 hrs post-dose)
  • 6. Patient background taken with uptake probe over knee
    • Knee has similar size and vascularity as the neck
    • Do NOT have lower leg hanging straight down (counts could shine through)
  • 7. Patient Thyroid CPM obtained — probe placed 25 cm from neck (constant for all counts)
  • 8. Patient returns at 24 hours from dosing
  • 9. 24-hour uptake same as 6-hour except standard counts do not have to be repeated

Percent Thyroid Uptake Formula

Normal Range Results

  • 4–6 hour uptake: 6%–18%
  • 24-hour uptake: 10%–30%

Thyroid Gland Imaging

131I, 123I & 99mTc all go to the thyroid. However, 123I & 99mTc remain the RPs of choice.

Why NOT to Use 131I for Thyroid Imaging

  • 1. Higher energy (364 keV)
  • 2. Lower doses required
  • 3. Less efficient (30%)

123I for Thyroid Imaging

  • Dose: 200–500 μCi in capsule or liquid form; patient NPO
  • Images taken @ 6 and/or 24 hours
  • Images: 50,000–100,000 counts (keep consistent)
  • Advantages: good energy for imaging; low radiation dose
  • Disadvantages: high cost; not as available

99mTc for Thyroid Imaging

  • 5–10 mCi injected IV
  • Images taken @ 20 minutes post-injection
  • Advantages: great physical characteristics; low radiation dose; higher doses; image sooner; easily available

Collimator Selection

  • Pinhole Collimator — gives high resolution for small organs; preferred for thyroid
  • LEAP Collimator — used for overall image showing head, neck, & chest

Thyroid Imaging Procedure

Indications:

  • 1. Determine size, shape & function of the thyroid
  • 2. Determine if a palpable nodule is functioning
  • 3. Locate ectopic thyroid tissue
  • 4. Determine if a mass is thyroid tissue

Patient Preparation:

  • 1. Same as thyroid uptake but not as strict
  • 2. NPO if using iodine
  • 3. Off thyroid-interfering meds if using iodine

Procedure:

  • 1. Dose the patient (give return instructions for RP used)
  • 2. Position patient supine with head tilted backward — towel under neck to hyperextend; keeps salivary glands from superimposing the thyroid
  • 3. Minimum 4 images:
    • a. ANT with anatomic marker over Suprasternal Notch (used for location)
    • b. ANT
    • c. RAO
    • d. LAO

Iodine-131 Whole Body Imaging

Introduction

  • 131I whole body imaging is performed when looking for metastatic thyroid disease or residual thyroid tissue
  • Iodine will go anywhere there is thyroid tissue
  • 131I allows for delayed imaging out to 2, 3, or 4 days (needed for good uptake and to find deep mets)

When is Whole Body Iodine Performed?

  • 1. Post-Thyroidectomy — patient has had thyroid removed (usually due to CA)
    • Looking for mets from primary thyroid cancer
    • Performed 1–2 months after thyroidectomy
  • 2. Post-Radioiodine Therapy — patient had 131I therapy to destroy their thyroid
    • Looking for any residual thyroid tissue

Procedure

Indications:

  • 1. R/O Thyroid CA mets
  • 2. Evaluate thyroid tissue after radioiodine therapy

Radiopharmaceutical: 131I — ingestion of 2–10 mCi

Patient Prep:

  • 1. Off thyroid hormones — ensures proper uptake
  • 2. No iodinated contrast
  • 3. NPO for 4 hours before and 1 hour after dosing

Procedure:

  • 1. Administer 131I
  • 2. Images as early as 24 hours; 48 & 72 hours offer better tumor-to-background ratio
  • 3. Patient positioned supine
  • 4. Whole body images ANT & POST (spot views: head, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, thighs)
  • 5. Images acquired for 5 min or 100,000 counts

Normal Physiologic Activity on Whole Body Iodine

  • Activity is expected in: mouth, stomach, bladder, salivary glands, & colon
  • Any activity outside these areas or in unexpected locations is suspicious for mets or residual thyroid tissue
20

Whole Body Iodine — Case Studies

Case Study #1

  • Patient had a total thyroidectomy and was scheduled for whole body iodine to R/O mets from history of thyroid CA
  • Administered 2 mCi of 131I; whole body scan images taken ANT and POST at 48 hours using whole body scanner
Image Findings — Case #1
  • Activity in the lungs — indicates lung mets
  • Activity in the neck region — surgery did not remove all of the thyroid
  • Normal activity expected in: mouth, stomach, bladder, salivary glands, colon

Case Study #2

  • 6-year-old female with prior Hx of thyroid CA; prior thyroidectomy; had three 25-mCi 131I therapy doses
  • Whole body iodine to evaluate residual thyroid tissue; ANT & POST images at 96 hours
Image Findings — Case #2
  • Activity in the neck — residual thyroid tissue
  • Activity in salivary gland, gastrointestinal tract, and bladder — normal physiologic activity
  • POST shows activity superior to thyroid — 131I excreted in sweat remaining in hair (confirmed by ponytail position)

Case Study #3

  • Patient with prior Hx of Thyroid CA; whole body iodine to evaluate thyroid CA mets; 5 mCi 131I; 48-hour ANT & POST
Image Findings — Case #3
  • Lungs: diffuse 131I activity — lung mets
  • Hot area in upper chest/neck — probable mets (not in thyroid position)
  • Spot in thoracic spine (POST view) — bone mets

Parathyroid Radiopharmaceuticals

Current radiopharmaceuticals for parathyroid imaging:

  • 1. 201Tl (Thallium-201)
  • 2. 99mTc Sodium Pertechnetate
  • 3. 99mTc-Sestamibi (MIBI)

201Tl in Parathyroid Imaging

  • Good imaging properties
  • General affinity for tumors
  • Half-time clearance from blood: 5 minutes
  • Peak uptake in parathyroid: 5–10 minutes
  • Localizes in tumors of the parathyroid
  • Also has normal uptake in the thyroid (causes overlap issues)
  • Dose: 2–3 mCi IV

99mTc in Parathyroid Imaging

  • Good imaging properties; naturally goes to normal thyroid tissue (good outline of thyroid)
  • Does NOT go to the parathyroid
  • Used in conjunction with 201Tl for dual-isotope subtraction

99mTc-Sestamibi (MIBI)

  • Naturally goes to:
    • 1. Normal thyroid tissue
    • 2. Thyroid adenomas
    • 3. Parathyroid adenomas
23

Parathyroid Imaging Procedure

Indications

  • Detect and localize the cause of hyperparathyroidism
  • Usually due to a parathyroid adenoma or parathyroid hyperplasia

Patient Prep

  • No patient prep required for a parathyroid scan

Procedure 1: Dual Isotope Subtraction

  • 1. Inject 2–3 mCi of 201Tl IV
  • 2. Patient supine — take a 10-minute pinhole image of thyroid
  • 3. Immediately after image, inject 10 mCi of 99mTc
  • 4. Take another 10-minute image post-99mTc injection
  • 5. Run subtraction program: subtract 201Tl from 99mTc

What to look for: 99mTc gives a good outline of the thyroid. 201Tl localizes in abnormal parathyroid tissue (adenomas). Any 201Tl concentration in the parathyroid area = positive finding.

Procedure 2: Single Isotope 2-Phase Method (MIBI)

  • 1. Inject 20 mCi 99mTc-Sestamibi IV
  • 2. Patient supine, neck hyperextended — 10-minute post-injection image
  • 3. LEAP collimator — 5-minute image
  • 4. Repeat images at 1½ & 3 hours

What to look for: Initial uptake in the thyroid should wash out by the delayed images. Any focal persistent MIBI uptake in the parathyroid on delayed images usually indicates an adenoma.

Parathyroid Imaging — Case Studies

Parathyroid Scan #1

  • Patient with increased levels of PTH; being evaluated for hyperparathyroidism
  • Administered 25 mCi of 99mTc-Sestamibi IV
  • 15-minute post-injection images ANT of head, neck, and upper chest
  • Images repeated at 2 hours for same anatomic views
Findings — Parathyroid Scan #1
  • Immediate views: normal accumulation in thyroid & parathyroid
  • Delayed views: incomplete washout — persistent focal uptake in R & L lower parathyroid glands
  • Bilateral parathyroid uptake → diagnosis: parathyroid hyperplasia (rather than an adenoma)

Parathyroid Scan #2

  • Patient with elevated serum Ca²⁺ & PTH; being evaluated for parathyroid adenoma
  • 22 mCi of 99mTc-Sestamibi IV; 20-minute and 2-hour delay images taken ANT
Findings — Parathyroid Scan #2
  • Initial images: normal uptake in thyroid; focal area of increased uptake in inferior right thyroid — suspicious
  • 2-hour delay: washout of most thyroid activity; persistent uptake in inferior right thyroid
  • Diagnosis: parathyroid adenoma — causing elevated PTH and hyperparathyroidism

Glossary

Endocrine System
System of ductless glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream to regulate body functions.
Exocrine Gland
A gland that secretes into ducts; does not produce hormones (e.g., sweat, oil, digestive glands).
Hormone
A chemical messenger released by an endocrine gland that travels through the blood to affect specific target cells.
Target Cells
Cells with specific receptors for a hormone; only these cells respond to that hormone.
Hypothalamus
The "master gland" located in the inferior central brain; links the nervous and endocrine systems; controls the pituitary.
Pituitary Gland (Hypophysis)
Pea-sized gland below the hypothalamus with anterior and posterior portions; secretes 7 major hormones from the anterior lobe.
TSH (Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone)
Pituitary hormone that stimulates the thyroid to produce and release T₃ and T₄.
TRH (Thyrotropin Releasing Hormone)
Hypothalamic hormone that stimulates the pituitary to release TSH; part of the negative feedback system.
Thyroid Follicles
Microscopic spherical sacs in the thyroid containing follicular cells (make T₃/T₄) and parafollicular cells (make calcitonin).
T₃ (Triiodothyronine)
Thyroid hormone containing 3 iodine atoms; more potent than T₄.
T₄ (Thyroxine)
Thyroid hormone containing 4 iodine atoms; secreted in greater quantities than T₃.
Thyroglobulin (TGB)
A protein of 5000 amino acids that serves as the template for thyroid hormone synthesis.
Graves Disease
Autoimmune hyperthyroidism; antibody mimics TSH causing excess thyroid hormone production; elevated RAI uptake (40–70%).
Goiter
Enlarged thyroid gland; symptom of many thyroid diseases including Graves disease.
Hot Nodule
A functioning thyroid nodule with increased radiopharmaceutical uptake; usually a hyperfunctioning adenoma.
Cold Nodule
A non-functioning thyroid nodule; 20% chance of malignancy; more common than hot nodules.
Lingual Thyroid
Thyroid tissue remaining at the base of the tongue after failing to migrate to its normal position.
PTH (Parathyroid Hormone)
Hormone secreted by the parathyroid gland; regulates calcium levels in the blood.
Hyperparathyroidism
Excess PTH production, usually from a parathyroid adenoma; causes elevated serum Ca²⁺ which can destroy tissues.
Tetany
Form of hypoparathyroidism; low Ca²⁺ causes spontaneous nerve activation and muscle twitching.
Aldosterone
Adrenal cortex steroid hormone; increases Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺/H⁺ excretion in the kidneys.
Addison's Disease
Hyposecretion of aldosterone; symptoms: anorexia, nausea, weight loss, muscular weakness; elevated K⁺, low Na⁺.
Cushing's Syndrome
Hypersecretion of adrenal cortex hormones; S&S: obesity, hypertension, hair growth.
¹³¹I Whole Body Scan
Nuclear medicine study to detect metastatic thyroid disease or residual thyroid tissue post-thyroidectomy or radioiodine therapy.
Neck Phantom
A phantom that mimics the neck and thyroid geometry; used with an uptake probe to count the standard during a thyroid uptake study.
Uptake Probe
A counting device used to measure the percent thyroid uptake of radioiodine at specified time points.
Dual Isotope Subtraction
Parathyroid imaging procedure using ²⁰¹Tl and ⁹⁹ᵐTc; the Tc image is subtracted from the Tl image to reveal parathyroid adenomas.
Single Isotope 2-Phase (MIBI)
Parathyroid imaging using ⁹⁹ᵐTc-Sestamibi; immediate and delayed images — persistent focal MIBI uptake at delay = adenoma.
⁹⁹ᵐTc-Sestamibi (MIBI)
Radiopharmaceutical that localizes in normal thyroid, thyroid adenomas, and parathyroid adenomas; ideal for single-isotope parathyroid study.
Pinhole Collimator
Specialized collimator providing high resolution for small organs; preferred for thyroid and parathyroid imaging.
