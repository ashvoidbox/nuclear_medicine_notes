What Interacts

Our first task is to determine how different types of radiation interact with matter.

  • Gamma-rays & x-rays
    • 5 ways they can interact with matter:
      • 1. Photoelectric effect
      • 2. Coherent Scatter
      • 3. Compton Scatter
      • 4. Pair Production
      • 5. Photodisintegration
  • Charged particles
    • LET
    • Range
    • Bremsstrahlung
    • Annihilation
  • Neutrons

When radiation interacts with matter, 2 things can happen:

1. Attenuation

  • A reduction in the amount of radiation as it passes through matter

2. Absorption

  • A certain amount of radiation is taken in to the matter

Attenuation and Absorption are kind of opposite to each other:

  • We have a 300 keV photon that interacts with matter and exits with 200 keV:
    • 100 keV was absorbed by the matter
    • The photon was attenuated by 100 keV

Passage Through Matter

Three quantities are associated with the passage of some forms of radiation through matter:

  1. Specific Ionizations (SI)
  2. Linear Energy Transfer (LET)
  3. Range

Specific Ionizations (SI)

  • Total number of ion pairs produced per unit length of the path of the incident radiation
    • The more ion pairs produced, the more damage is done
  • The amount of SI is dependent on:
    • Mass — greater mass → more interactions → more ion pairs → HIGHER SI
    • Charge — greatest effect on SI; higher charge increases interactions
    • Energy — SI increases with decreasing energy (lower energy photons go slower → more interactions; think of a magnet going slowly over paperclips)
  • An increase in SI will be seen toward the end of radiation's travel
  • The peak SI is called Bragg Ionization

Linear Energy Transfer (LET)

  • The amount of energy deposited (lost) per unit length of the path by the radiation
  • LET indicates the potential for biologically important damage from radiation
  • Formula: LET = SI × W
    • W = average energy required to produce an ion pair (expressed in keV)
    • LET is usually expressed as keV/mm

LET of radiation:

  • Electromagnetic Radiation (gamma & x-rays) and β-particles — lose small amount of energy per interaction → Low LET
  • Heavy Particles (α, neutrons, protons) — travel short distance, lose energy quickly → High LET

Some examples of LET in tissue:

  • A 3 MeV α-particle deposits 100 keV/μm

Range of Radiation

Range (R)

  • The straight distance traversed by a charged particle in the direction of travel
    • Expressed as mg/cm² of the absorber
  • Depends on:
    • Mass — heavier particles have a shorter range
    • Charge — higher charge → shorter range
    • Kinetic Energy — more energy → longer range
    • Density of absorber — more dense → shorter range

Path of travel:

  • α-particle — Travels in a straight line (unidirectional)
    • Heavy enough not to be deflected
    • Its range is a true representation of path traveled
  • β-particle — Travels in a multidirectional (tortuous) path
    • Lighter, gets deflected a lot
    • Its range is NOT a true representation of path traveled
  • The absorber thickness that reduces beam intensity by one half = the mean range

Gamma and X-rays Interact with Matter

Gamma Rays

Background info on gamma-rays:

  • Monoenergetic — emitted from the nucleus with a single energy
  • Penetrating — travel long path in absorber before losing all energy

When gamma-rays interact with matter:

  • Monoenergetic radiation attenuates exponentially
    • Photons are reduced by a consistent % per increment of thickness
    • The HVL is consistent (e.g., every 1 cm → 50% attenuated)

X-Rays

  • Polyenergetic — emitted with many energies
  • Penetrating radiation
  • Attenuates non-exponentially — reduced by a different % per increment (due to varying energies)
  • HVL is NOT consistent (e.g., one layer 30%, then 23%, then 21%)

Gamma-rays & x-rays can interact with:

  • The entire atom (low energy — long wavelength ≈ size of atom)
  • Just an orbital electron (middle energies)
  • Just the nucleus (high energies — like radiation therapy)

A photon can:

  • Pass through without interaction → Transmission
  • Be absorbed by the atom
  • Interact, lose some energy, change direction → Scatter

4 Factors Affecting Attenuation:

  1. Energy of photon — attenuation decreases as energy increases
  2. Density of matter — increases attenuation
  3. Atomic Number (Z#) — increasing Z# increases attenuation
  4. Electrons per gram — more electrons increases attenuation

5 Results of γ & X-ray Interaction:

  1. Photoelectric Effect
  2. Coherent Scatter
  3. Compton Scatter
  4. Pair Production
  5. Photodisintegration

Photoelectric Effect

  • A photon interacts with an electron of an atom
  • All energy of the photon is transferred to the electron — the photon no longer exists
  • If photon energy exceeds binding energy → electron is ejected = the photoelectron

Photoelectron Energy Formula

Eke = Eγ − Eβ
Eke = Kinetic energy of photoelectron  |  Eγ = Energy of incoming photon  |  Eβ = Binding Energy of electron

When and Where Photoelectric Effect Occurs

  • Most commonly in the K-shell (~75%)
  • L-shell ~20%
  • Remaining shells ~5%
  • Photons are more attracted to higher binding energies
  • Probability increases with atomic number: double Z# → probability increases by 2³ (×8)

Sequence of events:

  1. Photon strikes K-shell electron (most common)
  2. K-shell electron is ejected
  3. Atom left in excited state
  4. Electron from higher shell (usually L-shell) falls to fill vacancy
  5. A characteristic x-ray is emitted as the electron falls

Coherent Scatter

(Thompson or Rayleigh Scattering)

  • Occurs with low energy photons (below 10 keV)
  • Causes excitation rather than ionization
  • The photon interacts with an e⁻ of an atom and redirects in a different direction
    • Photon retains the same energy
    • Photon retains the same wavelength
  • This is not very common
Key point: No energy is lost — only the direction of the photon changes.
8

Compton Scatter

(classic)

  • Occurs in a range of energy photons: 10 keV – 10 MeV
  • A collision of a photon with a loosely bound outer shell electron
  • The photon ejects the electron (= the Compton/recoil electron)
  • The photon is deflected

Energy Distribution:

  • Low incident photon energy → scattered photon gets higher % of energy; Compton e⁻ gets less
    • e.g., 75 keV incident → ~65 keV to scattered photon
  • As incident photon energy increases → Compton e⁻ receives higher percentage

Examples:

  • 140 keV ⁹⁹ᵐTc photon strikes M-shell electron:
    • 91 keV → scattered photon | 49 keV → Compton electron
  • 1330 keV photon strikes M-shell electron:
    • 215 keV → scattered photon | 1115 keV → Compton electron

As the incident photon energy increases, the energy transferred to the Compton electron increases!!

9

Pair Production

  • Requires photon energy > 1.02 MeV
  • A photon interacts with the nucleus of an atom
  • Photon creates an e⁻ and e⁺ which are ejected
    • Each has a minimum energy of 0.511 MeV
    • Any energy above 1.02 MeV is distributed as kinetic energy between the two
  • The e⁻ moves off out of the atom
  • The e⁺ undergoes annihilation with a nearby e⁻ → produces two 511 keV photons
  • More common as atomic number increases
10

Photodisintegration

(photonuclear reaction)

  • Requires photon energy > 10 MeV
  • A very high energy photon interacts with the nucleus
  • If enough energy is transferred → a nucleon is emitted
  • A new nuclide is produced

Probability of Each Type of Interaction

The type of interaction depends on:

  1. Photon Energy
  2. Atomic number of absorber (Z#)

The 3 most important interactions for energy transfer:

  • Photoelectric Effect
  • Compton Interactions
  • Pair Production

Probability Summary

Interaction Effect of Z# increase Effect of Energy increase
Photoelectric Effect Probability increases Increases (up to ~0.15 MeV)
Compton Scattering Decreases (can still occur) Increases until ~1 MeV, then decreases
Pair Production Probability increases Increases (must be >1.02 MeV)

Particles Interact with Matter

Particles that interact with matter: α-particles, β-particles, neutrons

These particles interact with orbital electrons causing Ionization or Excitation.

Particle Excitation

  • Particle gives all or part of energy to the e⁻
  • Electron raises to higher energy state → emits characteristic x-rays when it falls back

Particle Ionization

  • Particle gives all or part of energy to the e⁻
  • Electron is ejected from its shell = Primary Electrons
  • If they have enough energy → they can cause further ionization/excitation
  • Secondary e⁻ that cause ionizations = delta (δ) rays

Bremsstrahlung

  • Occurs when a particle penetrates through the electron cloud and approaches the nucleus
  • Particle is repelled by the strong nuclear electrical forces → slows down rapidly
  • When the particle slows, it gives off an x-ray (Bremsstrahlung)

Probability of Bremsstrahlung increases with:

  • Higher kinetic energy of the particle (10 MeV e⁻ → 50%; 90 MeV e⁻ → 90%)
  • Higher atomic number of absorber (Air & Al = low Z# → minimal; Lead & Tungsten = high Z# → significant)
  • Lower mass of particle (β-particles >> α-particles)
⚠️ Storage of ³²P (Bremsstrahlung emitter):
Store in plastic FIRST, then lead around it.
→ Plastic: attenuates particles, minimal Bremsstrahlung
→ Lead: attenuates any x-rays from Bremsstrahlung
❌ Do NOT use lead alone — high Z# causes Bremsstrahlung x-rays that can escape!

Neutrons Interact with Matter

  • Neutrons have a neutral charge
  • Primarily interact with the nucleus — neutral charge allows them to enter the nucleus

3 Ways Neutrons Interact with Nuclei

  1. Elastic Scattering — the sum of kinetic energy of the neutron and nucleus is the same after interaction
  2. Inelastic Scattering — part of initial energy is used for excitation of the struck nucleus
  3. Neutron Capture — nucleus captures the neutron and creates a new nuclide

Relative Biological Effectiveness (RBE)

  • rem accounts for the difference in effectiveness of different types of radiation in causing biological damage
rem = rad × RBE
  • RBE = ratio of the dose of a standard radiation to produce a particular biological response
    • Determines the ionizing effects of radiation
    • More ionizations = more damage
    • Higher RBE = more ionizations produced
  • RBE varies with:
    • LET of the radiation
    • Radiation dose
    • Dose Rate
    • Biologic system being evaluated
15

Radiation Weighting Factors (Wr)

  • Used to describe the RBE for radiation protection purposes
rem = rad × Wr
  • Gives a numerical value to radiation biological effectiveness

Glossary of Terms

Attenuation
The reduction in intensity of radiation as it passes through matter, due to absorption and scattering.
Absorption
The process by which radiation energy is taken up by the absorbing material.
Specific Ionization (SI)
The total number of ion pairs produced per unit path length by ionizing radiation.
Linear Energy Transfer (LET)
The amount of energy deposited by ionizing radiation per unit length of path in matter (keV/μm).
Range (R)
The straight-line distance traversed by a charged particle in the direction of travel, expressed in mg/cm².
Half-Value Layer (HVL)
The thickness of an absorber required to reduce the intensity of a beam of radiation to half its original value.
Photoelectric Effect
Interaction in which a photon transfers all its energy to an orbital electron, ejecting it as a photoelectron. The photon ceases to exist.
Photoelectron
The electron ejected from an atom as a result of the photoelectric effect; its kinetic energy equals photon energy minus electron binding energy.
Binding Energy (Eβ)
The energy required to remove an electron from its shell. K-shell has the highest binding energy.
Coherent (Rayleigh) Scatter
Low-energy photon interaction (<10 keV) in which the photon is redirected with no loss of energy or change in wavelength.
Compton Scatter
Photon interaction (10 keV–10 MeV) with a loosely bound outer-shell electron; photon is deflected and loses some energy, ejecting a recoil electron.
Compton (Recoil) Electron
The outer-shell electron ejected during Compton scattering.
Pair Production
Photon interaction (>1.02 MeV) near a nucleus producing an electron–positron pair; requires minimum photon energy of 1.02 MeV.
Annihilation
When a positron collides with an electron, both are destroyed and two 511 keV photons are emitted in opposite directions.
Photodisintegration
High-energy photon (>10 MeV) interaction with a nucleus resulting in emission of a nucleon and creation of a new nuclide.
Bremsstrahlung
"Braking radiation" — X-rays produced when a charged particle decelerates near an atomic nucleus.
Delta (δ) Rays
Secondary electrons produced by primary ionization events that themselves have sufficient energy to cause further ionizations.
Excitation
A process in which an electron is raised to a higher energy state without being ejected from the atom.
Ionization
The process of removing an electron from an atom, creating an ion pair (positive ion + free electron).
Ion Pair
The positively charged atom and the freed electron produced when an electron is ejected from an atom.
Elastic Scattering (neutron)
Neutron–nucleus collision in which the total kinetic energy of the system is conserved.
Inelastic Scattering (neutron)
Neutron–nucleus collision in which some kinetic energy is used to excite the struck nucleus.
Neutron Capture
A nuclear reaction in which a nucleus absorbs a neutron and becomes a new nuclide (often radioactive).
Relative Biological Effectiveness (RBE)
The ratio of the dose of a reference radiation to the dose of the test radiation producing the same biological effect. Higher RBE = more damaging per unit dose.
rad
Unit of absorbed radiation dose; 1 rad = 0.01 J/kg of energy deposited in tissue.
rem
Roentgen Equivalent Man — a dose unit that accounts for biological effectiveness: rem = rad × RBE (or Wr).
Radiation Weighting Factor (Wr)
A dimensionless factor used in radiation protection to account for the biological effectiveness of different radiation types (replaces RBE in protection contexts).
Monoenergetic
Radiation emitted at a single, discrete energy level, as in gamma rays from nuclear transitions.
Nuclide
A specific atomic species defined by its number of protons and neutrons; different from an isotope, which refers to same-element variants.
Atomic Number (Z)
The number of protons in an atom's nucleus; determines the element and influences the probability of radiation interactions.
