Key Principle: Conservation in All Decay

In every radioactive decay mode, the following are conserved:

  • Energy
  • Charge
  • Mass

The Six Decay Modes

  1. Alpha (α) Decay
  2. Beta-Minus (β⁻) Decay
  3. Positron (β⁺) Decay
  4. Electron Capture (EC)
  5. Isomeric Transition (IT) — includes Gamma Emission and Internal Conversion
  6. Fission
A radionuclide can decay by one or a combination of these methods. When it decays, it transforms from an unstable parent nucleus into a more stable daughter nucleus.

Atomic Structure & Nuclear Stability

📖 Ch. 9

Basic Atomic Structure

  • Protons — positively charged particles in the nucleus; determine the element (atomic number Z)
  • Neutrons — neutral particles in the nucleus; together with protons they determine the mass number (A)
  • Electrons — negatively charged particles orbiting in shells outside the nucleus
Atomic Number (Z) = number of protons Mass Number (A) = protons + neutrons Neutrons (N) = A − Z

What Makes a Nucleus Unstable?

  • Radionuclides are unstable — they decay to become stable
  • Instability is driven by an imbalance between protons and neutrons:
    • Too many protons relative to neutrons → proton-rich nucleus → tends toward positron decay or electron capture
    • Too many neutrons relative to protons → neutron-rich nucleus (high n:Z ratio) → tends toward beta-minus decay
    • Very heavy nucleus → tends toward alpha decay or spontaneous fission

Parent → Daughter Transformation

  • The parent radionuclide is the original unstable nucleus
  • The daughter radionuclide is the more stable product
  • When decay occurs, the nucleus emits a particle, a gamma ray, or both
  • The atomic number (Z) and/or mass number (A) change depending on the decay mode
Henri Becquerel discovered natural radioactivity in 1896. Today, ~3,000 artificially produced radionuclides are known. We are concerned not with how they are made, but with how they decay.

Alpha Decay

📖 Ch. 9

When Does It Occur?

  • Occurs in radionuclides that are neutron deficient (proton rich) and typically very heavy
  • The parent has too many protons relative to neutrons

What Is Emitted?

  • An alpha particle (α) — a helium-4 nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons
  • Written as 42He or α
  • Charge: +2

How Does the Nucleus Change?

X → Y + α Mass number (A): decreases by 4 Atomic number (Z): decreases by 2

Properties of Alpha Particles

  • Size: Largest and heaviest type of radiation
  • Speed: Slowest — travels only a few centimeters in air
  • Penetration: Least penetrating — stopped by a sheet of paper or skin
  • Charge: +2
Alpha particles are the least penetrating externally but the most damaging internally if inhaled or ingested, due to their large size and high energy deposition over a short range.

Beta-Minus (β⁻) Decay

📖 Ch. 9

When Does It Occur?

  • Occurs in radionuclides that are neutron rich (high n:Z ratio)

What Happens Inside the Nucleus?

  • A neutron is converted into a proton inside the nucleus:
n → p + β⁻ + antineutrino

What Is Emitted?

  • A beta-minus particle (β⁻) — essentially an electron from the nucleus
  • An antineutrino — carries away excess energy from the reaction

How Does the Nucleus Change?

X → Y + β⁻ + antineutrino Mass number (A): does NOT change (lose neutron, gain proton) Atomic number (Z): increases by 1 (gained a proton)

Properties of Beta-Minus Particles

  • Size: Smaller than alpha; electron mass
  • Charge: −1
  • Speed: Faster than alpha, slower than gamma
  • Penetration: May travel a few meters in air, a few centimeters in skin; stopped by a few mm of aluminum

Positron (β⁺) Decay

📖 Ch. 9

When Does It Occur?

  • Occurs in radionuclides that are proton rich (high Z:n ratio)
  • Requires a minimum transition energy of 1.02 MeV

What Happens Inside the Nucleus?

  • A proton is converted into a neutron:
p → n + β⁺ + neutrino

What Is Emitted?

  • A positron (β⁺) — an electron with a positive charge (+1)
  • A neutrino — carries away excess energy

How Does the Nucleus Change?

X → Y + β⁺ + neutrino Mass number (A): does NOT change Atomic number (Z): decreases by 1

Annihilation — What Happens to the Positron?

  • The β⁺ flies out of the nucleus and quickly collides with a nearby electron (e⁻)
  • Annihilation occurs — mass is converted back to energy:
    • Two gamma photons are produced at 180° from each other
    • Each gamma has 0.511 MeV of energy (total = 1.02 MeV)

Why 1.02 MeV Minimum?

  • The mass of a proton and electron each equals ~0.511 MeV of energy
  • Creating a positron (pair production) requires converting at least 1.02 MeV of energy into mass
Positron decay and electron capture are competing alternatives for proton-rich nuclei. EC is favored when transition energy is less than 1.02 MeV; positron decay requires ≥ 1.02 MeV.
6

Electron Capture

📖 Ch. 9

When Does It Occur?

  • Occurs in radionuclides that are proton rich — same trigger as positron decay
  • Usually when transition energy is less than 1.02 MeV (positron decay is not energetically possible)

What Happens?

  • The nucleus captures an inner orbital electron (usually from the K-shell)
  • The captured electron combines with a proton to form a neutron:
p + e⁻ → n + neutrino

How Does the Nucleus Change?

Mass number (A): does NOT change Atomic number (Z): decreases by 1

What Follows EC?

  • Capturing the K-shell electron leaves a vacancy (hole) in the K-shell
  • An outer-shell electron falls in to fill the vacancy
  • The energy difference is released as a characteristic x-ray
Example: 201Tl (thallium-201) decays by EC and emits characteristic x-rays of mercury (Hg) at 69–83 keV. These x-rays are used diagnostically in nuclear medicine.

Isomeric Transition & Gamma Emission

📖 Ch. 9

Nuclear Energy States

  • Ground state — all protons and neutrons are at the lowest possible energy level
  • Isomeric states — the nucleus is in an excited (above-ground) energy level
    • Usually short-lived (seconds to minutes)
  • Metastable state — a long-lived isomeric state denoted by "m" (e.g., 99mTc)
    • Typically on the order of hours or more
    • 99mTc has a half-life of 6 hours

Two Forms of Isomeric Transition (IT)

  1. Gamma (γ) ray emission
  2. Internal Conversion (see Section 8)

Gamma Ray Emission

  • The nucleus in an excited isomeric state decays to a lower energy state
  • The energy difference is released as a gamma (γ) ray
  • Gamma rays come from the nucleus (not from electron shells)
Energy of γ = E(upper isomeric state) − E(lower isomeric state) Example: 300 keV state → 160 keV state = 140 keV gamma (This is why ⁹⁹ᵐTc emits a 140 keV gamma!)

How Does the Nucleus Change in IT?

  • Only the energy state changes — Z and A do not change
  • In a decay scheme, IT is shown as an arrow pointing straight down
Gamma emission and x-ray emission both produce electromagnetic radiation, but they originate from different places: γ rays come from the nucleus; characteristic x-rays come from electron shell transitions.

Internal Conversion & the Auger Process

📖 Ch. 9

Internal Conversion — The Process

  • This is the second form of isomeric transition
  • Instead of emitting a gamma ray, the nucleus transfers its excitation energy directly to an orbital electron (usually K-shell)
  • No gamma ray is produced in the process — the energy goes straight to the electron
  • If the excitation energy ≥ the electron's binding energy, the electron is ejected
    • The ejected electron is called the conversion electron

What Follows Ejection of the Conversion Electron?

  • A vacancy is left in the electron shell (usually K-shell)
  • An outer-shell electron falls in to fill the vacancy
  • A characteristic x-ray is emitted (same as after electron capture)

Key Distinction: IC vs. Gamma Emission

FeatureGamma EmissionInternal Conversion
Energy originNucleusNucleus
What is emittedGamma photonConversion electron
Gamma ray produced?YesNo
Followed by…Nothing (or further decay)Characteristic x-ray emission

The Auger Process

  • Can occur during internal conversion
  • After the conversion electron is ejected and an outer electron falls in, the energy released from that transition can strike yet another orbital electron
  • If that energy exceeds the electron's binding energy, the electron is ejected — this is the Auger electron
Remember: Internal conversion energy originates from the nucleus, but characteristic x-rays and Auger electrons result from rearrangements of orbital electrons.

Spontaneous Fission

📖 Ch. 9

When Does It Occur?

  • Occurs in very heavy nuclei (e.g., U-235, Cf-254)
  • Low probability overall; increases as mass number increases

What Happens?

  • The heavy nucleus spontaneously splits into:
    • 2 fission fragments
    • 2–3 neutrons
    • 200–400 MeV of excess energy
This is spontaneous fission — a natural decay mode. Man-made fission (in reactors) is a separate process covered in radiopharmacy.

Radiation Characteristics & Interactions

📖 Ch. 9 📖 Ch. 5

Particulate vs. Electromagnetic Radiation

  • Particulate radiation — carries mass and charge; directly ionizing
    • Alpha particles (α), Beta particles (β⁻, β⁺)
  • Electromagnetic radiation — no mass, no charge; indirectly ionizing
    • Gamma rays (γ), X-rays (x)

Comparison Table

PropertyAlpha (α)Beta (β⁻ / β⁺)Gamma (γ) / X-ray
TypeParticle (He nucleus)Particle (electron/positron)Electromagnetic photon
MassLargest (4 amu)Small (~1/1836 amu)None (massless)
Charge+2−1 (β⁻) / +1 (β⁺)None (neutral)
SpeedSlowestIntermediateSpeed of light
PenetrationLeast — stopped by skin/paperModerate — stopped by aluminum (few mm)Most — requires lead to stop
OriginNucleusNucleusNucleus (γ) or electron shells (x-ray)

Order of Penetration (Most → Least)

  1. Gamma rays and X-rays (equal — most penetrating; stopped by lead)
  2. Beta particles (stopped by a few mm of aluminum)
  3. Alpha particles (stopped by skin or paper)

Order of Speed (Fastest → Slowest)

  1. Gamma and X-rays (speed of light)
  2. Beta particles (fast, but not light speed)
  3. Alpha particles (slowest)
The larger the particle, the less penetrating it is. As particle energy increases, penetration increases for any given type.

Characteristic X-rays, Binding Energy & Half-Life

📖 Ch. 9

Electron Binding Energy & Shells

  • Electrons orbit the nucleus in shells (K, L, M, …)
  • The K-shell is closest to the nucleus and has the highest binding energy
  • Electrons in inner shells require more energy to be removed than outer-shell electrons

How Characteristic X-rays Are Produced

  • A vacancy is created in an inner shell (K-shell most commonly) — this happens after:
    • Electron Capture (the nucleus captures the K-shell electron)
    • Internal Conversion (the excitation energy ejects the K-shell electron)
  • An outer-shell electron falls in to fill the vacancy
  • The energy released = difference between the two shell energies → emitted as a characteristic x-ray
E(x-ray) = E(outer shell) − E(inner shell vacancy)
  • The energy is "characteristic" because it is specific to the element

Key Distinction

  • Gamma rays — energy originates from the nucleus
  • Characteristic X-rays — energy originates from electron shell rearrangements

Half-Life (T½)

  • The time it takes for a radioactive material to decay to 50% of its original activity
  • Radioactivity decreases over time
  • In a decay scheme, the half-life is listed next to the radionuclide
  • Example: 99mTc has a T½ of 6 hours
    • 100 mCi → 50 mCi after 6 hours → 25 mCi after 12 hours
Half-life reflects how quickly a radionuclide decays. It is shown in decay schemes and is an important property when choosing radiopharmaceuticals for patient studies.

Reading Decay Schemes

📖 Ch. 9

What a Decay Scheme Shows

  1. Mode of decay — how the radionuclide decays, % abundance, and energy
  2. Byproducts of decay — particles or photons emitted
  3. Half-life — listed next to the parent nuclide

Arrow Directions in Decay Schemes

Arrow DirectionMeaningDecay Type
Slants to the rightZ increases (gaining a proton)β⁻ Decay
Slants to the leftZ decreases (losing a proton)β⁺ Decay or EC
Points straight downZ unchanged (energy change only)Isomeric Transition (IT)

Other Information Shown

  • The percentage alongside each arrow indicates how often that decay path occurs
  • The energy value shown is the energy of the particle or photon emitted
  • Gamma transitions are shown as vertical (downward) arrows from excited states to lower states
Decay schemes are a compact visual language. An arrow to the right = more protons = β⁻. An arrow to the left = fewer protons = β⁺ or EC. A vertical arrow = no change in Z = IT (gamma or internal conversion).

Glossary

📖 Ch. 9 📖 Ch. 5
Alpha Decay
Nucleus emits an alpha particle (⁴₂He). Z decreases by 2, A decreases by 4. Occurs in heavy, proton-rich nuclei.
Alpha Particle
2 protons + 2 neutrons. Charge +2. Largest, slowest, least penetrating; stopped by skin or paper.
Annihilation
A positron (β⁺) collides with an electron (β⁻). Their mass converts to two 0.511 MeV gamma photons emitted at 180° to each other.
Antineutrino
Antimatter counterpart of a neutrino; emitted in beta-minus decay to carry away excess energy.
Atomic Number (Z)
Number of protons in the nucleus. Determines the element. Changes in all decay modes except IT.
Auger Electron
Electron ejected when characteristic x-ray energy from a shell transition ejects another orbital electron instead of being emitted as a photon.
Beta-Minus Decay (β⁻)
Neutron-rich nucleus converts n → p + β⁻ + antineutrino. Z increases by 1; A unchanged.
Characteristic X-ray
X-ray emitted when an outer electron fills an inner-shell vacancy. Energy = difference between the two shell binding energies. Originates from electron shells (not the nucleus).
Conversion Electron
Orbital electron ejected during internal conversion when nuclear excitation energy is transferred directly to it (instead of being emitted as a gamma ray).
Daughter Radionuclide
The more stable product nucleus after radioactive decay of the parent.
Decay Scheme
Diagram showing decay mode, arrow direction, energy, % abundance, and half-life of a radionuclide.
Electron Capture (EC)
Proton-rich nucleus captures an inner-shell (K-shell) electron. p + e⁻ → n + neutrino. Z decreases by 1; A unchanged. Produces characteristic x-rays.
Excitation Energy
Energy above ground state that a nucleus holds in an isomeric state. Released as a gamma ray (IT) or transferred to an orbital electron (IC).
Ground State
Lowest energy state of a nucleus; all nucleons at their lowest possible energy levels.
Half-Life (T½)
Time for radioactive activity to decay to 50% of its original value. Listed in decay schemes. Example: ⁹⁹ᵐTc T½ = 6 hours.
Internal Conversion (IC)
Form of isomeric transition where nuclear excitation energy ejects an orbital electron (conversion electron) rather than emitting a gamma ray. Followed by characteristic x-ray emission.
Isomeric Transition (IT)
Nucleus in excited metastable state returns to ground state by gamma emission or internal conversion. Z and A do not change.
Mass Number (A)
Total number of protons + neutrons. Unchanged in β⁻, β⁺, EC, and IT. Decreases by 4 in alpha decay.
Metastable State
Long-lived isomeric state denoted by "m" (e.g., ⁹⁹ᵐTc). Typically lasts hours or more.
Neutrino
Nearly massless, chargeless particle emitted in positron decay and electron capture; carries away excess energy.
Parent Radionuclide
The original unstable nucleus that undergoes radioactive decay.
Positron (β⁺)
Antimatter electron with charge +1. Emitted in positron decay; undergoes annihilation with a nearby electron producing two 0.511 MeV gamma photons.
Positron Decay (β⁺)
Proton-rich nucleus converts p → n + β⁺ + neutrino. Requires ≥1.02 MeV. Z decreases by 1; A unchanged.
Spontaneous Fission
Heavy nucleus splits into 2 fragments + 2–3 neutrons + 200–400 MeV energy. Probability increases with mass number.
