Introduction: Biologic Response to Radiation

The body has a particular response to radiation:

  • Effects to radiation are either Stochastic or Nonstochastic
  • Organs and Tissues have individual responses
  • Systems have responses
  • The total body has a response to radiation
  • Their are long term effects of radiation to the body
  • A fetus has a response to radiation
    • Phases of development have different effects

Stochastic vs. Nonstochastic Effects

Stochastic vs. Nonstochastic

Biologic effects of irradiation are either Stochastic vs. Nonstochastic:

  • Stochastic (statistical response)
    • Effects occur randomly in Nature
  • Non-Stochastic (deterministic)
    • Effects are determined

Stochastic Effects:

Stochastic effects occur randomly in nature:

  • Effects that occur by chance
    • Consist primarily of cancer and genetic effects
      • Keep in mind that it is not certain that any effects will occur
  • They are non-threshold
    • No specific dose produces effects
    • It can never be determined for certain that an occurrence of cancer or genetic damage was due to a specific exposure
    • Radiation damage to a single cell or many cells can produce effects
  • Low dose radiation can produce disease effects as well as high dose
    • However the probability of effects increases with increasing dose
  • Effects are characterized by long-term, low-level exposure to radiation
    • Often show up years after exposure

Stochastic Effects Curve

Stochastic Effects are based on a Linear-quadratic dose-response curve

  • The rate of change in response is different at different doses
  • The response may change slowly at low doses but rapidly at high doses
    • Low doses show a Linear response
      • There is no change to effects as Radiation increases
      • This is only at low radiation
    • High doses show a Quadratic response
      • Effects increase as dose increases

Examples of Stochastic Effects

  • Nuclear Medicine gives low doses to patients
    • Effects from Nuc Med are considered Stochastic
    • Low doses could produce effects
  • Other Examples of Stochastic Effects:
    • Radiation induced hereditary effects
      • A single DNA mutation causes hereditary effects
    • Radiation induced cancer incidences
      • Any amounts of radiation can produce cancer
  • The ALARA principle is based on Stochastic Effects
    • Keep doses low to keep the probability of effects low

 Non-Stochastic Effects:

  • They have a threshold
    • Doses below a limit produce no effects
    • Have a clear relationship between the exposure and the effect
  • Based on High Radiation Dose
    • These doses cause a specific effect
    • Effects are based on severity and not probability
      • We know effects are produced it's a matter of how bad they are
  • Severity of Effects produce a linear response to dose
    • As dose increases the severity of effects increases
    • The magnitude of the effect is directly proportional to the size of the dose
  • Characterized by short-term, but high-level exposure to radiation

Non-Stochastic Effects Curve:

Non-Stochastic Effects are based on a Sigmoid Dose Response Curve:

  • S-shaped curve
  • Has a particular threshold
    • The area where effects will start
  • There is a non-linear relationship b/w effect and dose
    • The severity is not proportional to dose

 Examples of Non-Stochastic Effects

  • A certain response is produced by certain dose of radiation
    • Cataracts
    • Erythema
    • Fibrosis
    • Hematopoietic damage
    • Sterility

Specific Nonstochastic Effects on certain systems

  • Hemopoietic Syndrome - medical conditions that affect the blood
    • Conditions appear after a gamma dose of about 200 rads (2 Gy)
      • Ablation of the bone marrow
      • Nausea and vomiting within several hours after the overexposure occurred
      • Effects can be reversed up until an exposure of about 700 rads (7 Gy) or greater
  • Gastrointestinal Syndrome - stomach and the intestines
    • Condition follows a total body gamma dose of about 1000 rads (10 Gy) or greater
      • Intestinal epithelium will shed off
      • Usually death within 1 - 2 weeks
  • Central Nervous System
    • A total body gamma dose in excess of about 2000 rads (20 Gy)
      • Unconsciousness withing minutes and usually death within hours - days
        • Higher the dose the quicker death will occur
  • Skin
    • Because of its physical location, the skin is subject to more radiation exposure
    • A dose of 100 - 1000 rads will effect the skin
      • Early effects to radiation include:
        • Erythema (reddening)
        • Inflammation
        • Desquamating (Peeling)
        • These effects will usually repair themselves
    • High doses of radiation to the skin (>2,000 rads):
      • Produce the same early effects but more severe Late effects
      • Atrophy Changes in pigmentation
      • Necrosis (tissue death)
      • Alopecia (hair loss)
  • Reproductive Organs
    • A single dose of only 30 rads (300 mGy) to the testes results in temporary sterility among men
    • A 300 rad (3 Gy) dose to the ovaries produces temporary sterility
    • Higher doses increase the period of sterility
  • Eyes response to Radiation:
    • The lens of the eye is very sensitive to radiation
    • When damaged cells accumulate
      • They become cataracts
      • Main radiation effect to the eye
        • Cataracts have a threshold dose 200 rads (2 Gy)
          • Will cause cataracts in some people
        • 700 rads will cause cataracts in everyone

Radiosensitivity in Cells

Radiosensitivity in Cells

  • Cells, tissues and organs have different responses to radiation:
    • Radiosensitive means it is sensitive to radiation
      • Doesn't take as much radiation to cause damage
    • Radioresistant means it is resistant to radiation
      • Takes more radiation to cause damage

Cells in the Body

  • There are basically two types of cells found in tissues in the body:
    • Differentiated
      • Have a specific physiologic function
      • Cells that are specialized for a particular function
        • Blood cells, muscles, heart cells
    • Undifferentiated
      • Do not have a specific physiologic function
      • They are cells that reproduce to replace lost cells

Different types of cells and their radiosensitivity

Law of Bergonié & Tribondeau

Law of Bergonie & Tribondeau

  • The "Law of Bergonie & Tribondeau" predicts sensitivity of these cells
    • Cells tend to be radiosensitive if they have three properties:
      • Cells have a high division rate
      • Cells have a long dividing future
      • Cells are of an unspecialized type
  • This tells us that:
    • Tissues which are young and rapidly growing are most likely radiosensitive
    • Undifferentiated cells are more radiosensitive
      • Especially during mitosis
  • Radiosensitivity of living tissues varies with maturation and metabolism
    1. Stem cells are radiosensitive
      • More mature cells are more resistant
    2. Younger tissues are more radiosensitive
    3. Tissues with high metabolic activity are highly radiosensitive
    4. High proliferation and growth rate, high radiosensitive

Total Body Response to Radiation

Total Body response to Radiation:

  • The effects of radiation are characterized by the survival time
    • LD50/60 is the most common survival time
      • Lethal Dose that kills 50% of a species in 60 days
  • LD50/60 for humans is 400 - 600 rads
    • LD50/60 for dogs is 300 rads
    • LD50/60 for mice is 900 rads

Acute Radiation Syndrome

Acute Radiation Syndrome

Total Body irradiation can result in Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS)

  • 200 - 300 rads is required to see ARS
  • ARS appears in four stages
    • Prodromal - period between the appearance of initial symptoms and full development
    • Latent - existing but not yet developed or manifested
    • Manifest illness
      • Hematopoietic Syndrome
      • GI Syndrome
      • CNS Syndrome
    • Recovery or Death
  • The length of each stage is dose dependent
    • The lower the dose the longer time is spent in each stage

Prodromal & Latent Stages:

  • Prodromal Stage:
    • Exhibits several symptoms
      • Nausea
      • Vomiting
      • Diarrhea
  • Latent Stage:
    • The individual appears to be symptom free
    • In reality, changes are taking place that will either lead to recovery or death

Manifest Stage:

  • Individual becomes ill and
  • Radiation syndromes appear as a result of the damage to organs
    • The syndromes that appear are dose dependant
      • Hematopoietic Syndrome
      • GI Syndrome
      • CNS Syndrome
  • Recovery or Death Stage:
    • Individual either recovers or dies

Fetal Response to Radiation

Fetal Response to Radiation

 A fetus has specific responses to radiation:

  • Overall most radiation exposure events will not expose the fetus
  • The responses depend on:
    • Total Dose
      • Higher dose gives a more severe response
    • Rate of Dose
      • Higher Dose Rate gives a more severe response
    • Quality of radiation
      • Particulate radiation will have a more severe response
    • Stage of development

A fetus undergoes 3 stages of Development

  • Pre-implantation
  • Organogenesis
  • Fetal (growth)

Each stage presents different responses to radiation

Pre-Implantation stage

  • Conception to 10 days
  • The response to radiation in this stage is:
    • Usually all or nothing
      • Prenatal death
      • No response B/c the cells heal themselves

Organogenesis Stage:

  • Major Organogenesis occurs from 2 - 8 weeks
  • Radiation is a known teratogen during this stage
    • A teratogen is an agent that causes birth defects
  • Birth defects caused during this period from radiation
    • Cataracts
    • Childhood Leukemia
    • Mental Retardation
    • Microcephaly
    • Growth retardation
    • Skeletal defect

Fetal Growth Stage:

  • Occurs from 8 weeks to term
  • A fetus in this stage is fairly radioresistant
  • It takes a high dose of radiation to cause effects
  • Possible effects:
    • Growth Retardation
    • Future cancer

Tolerance Doses

Tolerance Doses

  • The Tolerance Dose is the dose where complications are seen in an organ:
  • Low Tolerance Dose (10-20 Gy)
    • Gonads
    • Lens of eye
    • Developing organs
  • Moderate (20-45 Gy)
    • Stomach & intestines
    • Liver, kidneys & heart
  • High (50-70 Gy)
    • Oral cavity, esophagus, rectum, salivary glands
    • Mature bone & cartilage
    • CNS
  • Very High (>75 Gy)
    • Ureters & urethra
    • Mature muscle & blood

Radiation Doses

    • The dose rate at which the dose is given
    • Where the dose is supposed to go
    • What organ gets the most radiation
    • How much radiation organs get

Dose Rate

    • Rad/time
      • Rad/min
      • Gy/sec
  • The rate at which radiation is delivered

Dose Rate Effect

Dose Rate Effect

  • The dose rate can effect the radiosensitivity to cells
    • Factor in the amount of cellular damage
    • Factor in the response of cells to radiation
  • The dose rate can determine the amount of damage
    • The higher rate of the dose the greater the damage
      • Double strand breaks of DNA
      • Repair of cells is less likely due to less time to repair
  • The lower rate of the dose the lesser the damage
    • Single strands breaks of DNA
    • Cells usually have time to repair themselves
  • Dose rate effect has been observed in many types of damage:
    • Chromosome Aberrations
      • More aberrations with higher dose rate
    • Division delay
      • Cells may be inhibited with higher dose rate
    • Reproductive failure
      • Cells die with higher dose rates and that stops cells reproduction
    • Survival times
      • Cell survival is shorter with higher dose rate

Glossary

Stochastic Effects
Random, probabilistic radiation effects (e.g., cancer, genetic mutations). Non-threshold — any dose can theoretically cause effects. Probability increases with dose.
Non-Stochastic (Deterministic) Effects
Dose-dependent, predictable radiation effects with a threshold. Severity increases with dose (e.g., cataracts, erythema, radiation syndrome).
Radiosensitive
Describes cells or tissues that are sensitive to radiation damage; do not require much radiation to cause injury.
Radioresistant
Describes cells or tissues that are resistant to radiation damage; require higher doses to produce injury.
Differentiated Cells
Specialized cells with a specific physiologic function (e.g., blood cells, muscle cells). Generally more radioresistant.
Undifferentiated Cells
Non-specialized cells that reproduce to replace lost cells (stem cells). Generally more radiosensitive.
Law of Bergonié & Tribondeau
Cells are more radiosensitive if they have a high division rate, a long dividing future, and are of an unspecialized type.
LD50/60
Lethal Dose that kills 50% of a species within 60 days. For humans: 400–600 rads.
Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS)
A syndrome caused by whole-body irradiation ≥200–300 rads. Four stages: Prodromal → Latent → Manifest Illness → Recovery or Death.
Hematopoietic Syndrome
ARS syndrome affecting blood-forming organs (bone marrow). Appears at doses of 200–1000 rads.
GI Syndrome
ARS syndrome affecting the gastrointestinal tract. Appears at higher doses than hematopoietic syndrome.
CNS Syndrome
ARS syndrome affecting the central nervous system. Appears at very high doses; almost always fatal.
Teratogen
An agent that causes birth defects. Radiation is a known teratogen during the organogenesis stage of fetal development (2–8 weeks).
Organogenesis Stage
Fetal development stage from 2–8 weeks during which major organs form. Most radiosensitive period; radiation can cause cataracts, leukemia, microcephaly, and growth retardation.
Tolerance Dose
The radiation dose at which complications begin to appear in a specific organ. Ranges from low (10–20 Gy for gonads/lens) to very high (>75 Gy for mature muscle).
Dose Rate
The delivery of radiation dose per unit time (e.g., rad/min, Gy/sec).
Dose Rate Effect
The influence of dose rate on radiosensitivity. Higher dose rate → greater damage (less repair time). Lower dose rate → less damage (more repair time).
Source Organ
The organ in which a radiopharmaceutical localizes over time; the organ that emits radiation in an NM procedure.
Target Organ
The organ of interest in a nuclear medicine procedure — where you want the radiopharmaceutical to go (e.g., bone in a bone scan).
Critical Organ
The organ receiving the highest radiation dose in a nuclear medicine procedure. It dictates the maximum administered dose for that study.
