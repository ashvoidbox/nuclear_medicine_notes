Statistics

Statistics are important in Nuclear Medicine:

  • Decay is a random process
  • Therefore measuring radioactivity can be difficult
  • Statistics give us an accurate way to represent radioactivity

We will deal with statistics related to the counting of radioactivity

So, decay is a random process:

  • We can't predict exactly how many radioactive atoms will decay from a RN
    • The amount will fluctuate slightly
  • We also can't predict which radioactive atom will decay and at what time
  • We come up with an average value for the amount of radioactive atoms that decay per time
  • We need to determine how the fluctuations in the decay of radioactive atoms affect the result that we come up with
  • The difference between the actual (True Value) and the theoretical (Value we come up with) is our error in measurement

Error in Measurement:

  • There are 2 types of errors in measurement
    1. Systematic Errors
      • Come from malfunctioning or miscalibrated machines
      • The machines can Overestimate counting or Underestimate counting
      • These errors can be solved by fixing the machine
    2. Random Errors
      • Caused by uncontrollable factors
        • High Voltage Fluctuations, extraneous radiation sources etc...
      • These errors may be a one-time deal and may correct themselves or you may have to fix the situation

Mean

Radiation counting can have a mean ():

  • Radionuclides decay randomly
  • Therefore, it's good to have a mean count when counting radiation
    • Mean is an average value
  • Calculating mean is simple -= x / N
    • Take a series of counts (usually around 10)
      • # of times you counted your sample = N
    • Add the values of all the measurements (x)
    • Divide x by N

Mode

Radiation counting can also have a mode:

  • The value occurring most often
    • Rather than the average
  • Consider the following 1 second counts from a radioactive source
    • 25, 20, 25, 26, 19, 29, 25, 24, 28
  • What is the mode for the above counts?
    • Remember it's the count occurring most often
      • That would be 25

Radiation counting can also have a median:

  • The middle value of the several readings
    • Rather than the average
  • Consider the following 1 second counts from a radioactive source
    • 25, 20, 18, 30, 19, 29, 35, 24, 28
  • What is the median for the above counts?
    • First; you need to rank the counts in order
      • 18, 19, 20, 24, 25, 28, 29, 30, 35
    • The middle value is 25

Accuracy & Precision

Accuracy and Precision are related to Radiation Counting:

  • Accuracy - Indicates how close your measurements are to a true value
    • If there are 12,000 true actual counts
      • 11,995 counts is pretty accurate
      • 8,500 counts is not very accurate
    • Used in describing systematic errors
  • Precision - Indicates the reproducibility of your measurements
    • If a 1 mCi 99mTc source is counted for 1 minute and produces 1,000 actual counts
    • Remember radiation decays randomly
      • It won't always be 1,000 counts
      • 1,000 counts should be your average
    • If the 1 mCi 99mTc source is counted 5 times
    • Measurements that has high precision (is precise) is:
      • 1005, 995, 1000, 994, 1004
        • The range of the counts was low
    • Measurements that has low precision (not precise) is:
      • 1205, 1305, 1300, 1000, 705
        • The range of the counts was high
    • Used in describing random errors

Can measurements be both accurate and precise???

  • You can be Precise but not Accurate
    • If a 99mTc source produces 20,000 actual counts
    • You count that source 5 times
      • You get: 17,140, 17,005, 17,250, 17,190, & 17,090
        • That is precise - all counts are similar
      • However you are not accurate because your average is 17,135
        • That is far from the 20,000 actual counts
  • You can be Accurate but not Precise
    • If a 99mTc source produces 20,000 actual counts
    • You count that source 5 times
      • You get: 25000, 20,005, 15250, 20,190, & 20,090
        • That is not precise - all counts are not similar
      • However you are accurate because your average is 20107
        • That is close from the 20,000 actual counts

Standard Deviation

Standard Deviation (σ):

  • Tells you how tightly the events are clustered around the mean in a set of data
  • In a set of data how close is the data to the mean

How is Standard Deviation (σ) represented :

  • When the Data is closely packed around the mean the standard deviation is said to be small
    • ex. 50, 52, 51, 50, 49, 52, 48
  • When the Data is varied and spread out the s is large
    • ex. 50, 75, 25, 29, 30, 81, 85
  • We want our gamma camera's to have a low Standard Deviation

Standard Deviation can tell us how close we are to a true value

  • The graph below shows σ from a true value:

  • One standard deviation (1σ) away from the mean in either direction on the horizontal axis (the red area on the above graph)
    • Accounts for somewhere around 68 percent of the events in this group
  • Two standard deviations away from the mean (the red and green areas)
    • Account for roughly 95 percent of the events
  • Three standard deviations (the red, green and blue areas)
    • Account for about 99 percent of the events

How does this relate to Radiation Counting?

  • When counting (n) a radioactive source:
    • n ± 1σ
      • There is a 68% chance that your value will be within the range of the mean
      • This is a confidence interval of 68%
    • n ± 2σ
      • There is a 95% chance that your value will be within the range of the mean
      • This is a confidence interval of 95%
    • n ± 3σ
      • There is a 99% chance that your value will be within the range of the mean
      • This is a confidence interval of 99%

So, what is percent Standard Deviation?

  • Percent standard deviation (%σ):
    • Helps to analyze counting data
      • Determines the statistical error
    • The formula:
      • %σ = 100 /

Nuclei

Nuclei

  • OK; just a quick review:
    • Basically the amount of radioactive atoms in a radionuclide
    • When the nuclei decay they are disintegrations
    • The number of nuclei affects:
      • Decay Constant (λ) - Disintegration Constant
        • Fraction of the atoms that decay per unit time
      • Decay Rate

Lets define some variables and look at a formula related to Nuclei:

  • N = # of RA nuclei
  • n = # of RA nuclei that decay over time (t)
  • Therefore: Decay Rate (DR) = N / t = n
  • Now let's compare the 3 variables:
      • As we get more nuclei the number of nuclei that decay increase
      • If the number of nuclei that decay increase the Decay Rate increases
15

Decay Rate

So, what is the Decay Rate:

  • Decay Rate = λN
    • DR = Decay Constant Number of Nuclei
    • DR = Fraction of nuclei that decay per unit time TIMES the number of nuclei
    • DR = 1/t N = N/t = Nt-1
      • Therefore t-1 = per time
      • Nuclei per time

Lets look at the decay constant (λ)a little closer...

  • λ = Fraction of nuclei that decay per unit time
    • - hr-1, min-1 , sec-1
      • per hour, per minute, per second
  • If λ = 0.01 per minute
    • = 0.01 / minute
    • = 0.01 min-1
    • = 1%

Lets compare a couple of decay constants...

  • λ = 0.01 hr-1
    • = 1% of the nuclei decay per hour
      • Therefore if you have 10,000 nuclei, after 1 hour you have 9,900 nuclei
        • Q 1% of 10,000 is 100
  • λ = 0.1 hr-1
    • = 10% of the nuclei decay per hour
      • Therefore if you have 10,000 nuclei, after 1 hour you have 9,000 nuclei
        • 10% of 10,000 is 1000
  • λB decays faster and has a shorter half-life than λA

Lambda

Lambda (λ):

  • You usually don't know what l is but you usually know the radionuclide
  • λ = 0.693 / T1/2
    • T1/2 is the Half-Life of the radionuclide you are dealing with
  • Therefore DR = λN
    • = (0.693 / T1/2)(N)

Let's work a Decay Rate problem but this time we don't know what the Decay Rate is

  • A source of Tc-99m contain 2.5 X 1012 Nuclei. Find the Decay Rate (dpm) and activity (mCi).

      • λ = 0.693 / (6 hr)(60 min/hr) = 1.925 X 10-3 min-1
        • You must multiply 6 hr by 60 minutes to convert hours to minutes
    • DR = (1.925 X 10-3 min-1)(2.5 X 1012 Nuclei)
      • = 4.8 X 109 dpm
    • Activity =
      • = 2.17 mCi
    • This radionuclide is decaying at a rate of 4.8 X 109 Nuclei every minute and it contains 2.17 mCi

Decaying Nuclei

 Decaying Nuclei

  • So we know that:
    • All radionuclides have a certain number of nuclei
    • Those nuclei decay
    • We need to find out how many nuclei we have after a certain amount of time
  • Lets define a couple of variables:
    • No - total number of nuclei at starting time (t=0)
    • N - number of nuclei remaining at any time (t)
  • Fraction remaining of the number of nuclei:
    • N / No

Formula to decay nuclei (decay equation):

  • N = No e-λt
    • N - number of nuclei remaining at any time (t)
      • This what we are looking for
    • No - total number of nuclei at starting time (t=0)
    • e - Euler's Constant (2.718)
    • λ = 0.693 / T1/2
    • t - time that the nuclei decayed
  • This formula gives the number of nuclei remaining after time 0
    • e-λt is the post decay factor
      • Decay Factors have no units

Decay Factor (DF) Problems:

  • Find the Decay Factor for I-125 at t = 10 days.
  • Post Decay Factor = e-λt
  • λ = 0.693 / 60 days = 0.01155 dy-1
  • DF = e-(0.01155 dy-1)(10days) = 0.88
  • Therefore there is about 88% of I-125 remaining after 10 days
  • Note to Remember:
    • Always make sure your units match up
      • In the above problem dy-1cancelled out days
      • DF has no units, they should always cancel out

Decay Equation

Decay equation:

  • You may not always know the DF
  • You may have to use the Decay Equation to decay activity
  • Decay Equation: A = Ao e-λt
    • A - Activity remaining at any time (t)
    • Ao - Activity at starting time (t=0)
    • e-λt - Decay Factor

How you decay activity using the decay equation?

  • ~ A = Ao e-λt
    1. Calculate your Decay Constant - λ = 0.693 / T1/2
    2. Calculate your Decay Factor e-λt
    3. Multiply your decay factor by the original activity
    4. Your answer is your new activity that has decayed after time t

Decay equation Problems:

  • Determine the activity remaining after 8 hours if you started with 25 mCi of 99mTc.
  • A = Ao e-λt
    1. λ = 0.693 / 6 hr = 0.1155 hr-1
    2. Calculate your Decay Factor e-λt = e-(0.1155hr-1)(8 hrs) = 0.40
    3. Multiply your decay factor by the original activity - 25 mCi * 0.4 = 10 mCi
    4. Your answer is your new activity that has decayed after time t

Post Decay vs. Pre Decay

Post Decay vs. Pre Decay...

  • So far we have figured out how to decay activity and determine what it remaining after time=0
    • This is our Post Decay
  • We can also figure out how much activity you had before time=0
    • This is our Pre Decay
  • Regardless of which one we use you have to use a Decay Factor

Lets go from Pre to Post and Post to Pre Decay....

  • You can easily go from Post to Pre Decay Factor:
    • 1/Post DF = Pre-Decay Factor
    • A Post DF of 0.8909 = 1.122 Pre-Decay Factor
      • 1/0.8909 = 1.122
      • The 1 hr post DF for 99mTc is 0.8909
      • The 1 hr pre DF for 99mTc is 1.122
  • You can easily go from Pre to Post Decay Factor:
    • 1/Pre DF = Post-Decay Factor
    • A Pre DF of 1.01 = 0.989
      • 1/1.01 = 0.989
      • The 1 hr pre DF for 99Mo is 1.01
      • The 1 hr post DF for 99Mo is 0.989

Lets compare the Decay Factors of Post Decay, Pre Decay, and time=0......

Why are the decay factors like this:

  • Remember you are multiplying the DF by the activity:
    • Post Decay - Will be < 1 b/c the activity is decreasing
    • Time = 0 - Will be = 1 b/c the activity hasn't decayed
    • Pre Decay - Will be > 1 b/c the activity is greater than time = 0

So, what is the Pre-decay factor...

  • First look at the Post DF again - e-λt
  • Now look at the Pre DF - eλt
  • What's different b/w the 2 DF
    • Pre Decay is + and Post Decay is -
  • So then the Pre Decay Equation is
    • Ao = A eλt

Let's look at a Pre-decay factor problem

  • A vial of 131I arrives at noon on Monday. It's calibrated (assayed) at 5 mCi noon on Tuesday (assay date). What is the activity on arrival?
    • Pre Decay Equation is - Ao = A eλt
    • What can we say about this problem
      • We have 5 mCi at noon on Tuesday
      • We want to know how much we had at noon on Monday
        • 24 hrs earlier (1 day earlier)
        • I used 1 day to keep the units equal with the 8 day Half Life of 131I
    • Ao = 5 mCi e(.693/8dy)(1 day)
      • = (5 mCi) (1.09)
      • = 5.45 mCi

Glossary of Terms

Statistics (Nuclear Medicine)
The mathematical study of random processes such as radioactive decay. Used to describe and predict the behavior of radionuclide counting data.
Mean (x̅)
The arithmetic average of a set of values. Calculated by dividing the sum of all values by the number of values (x̅ = Σx / N).
Mode
The value that occurs most often in a data set. In a perfectly symmetrical Bell Curve, mode = mean = median.
Median
The middle value in a ranked data set. When values are arranged in order, the median is the central value.
Bell Curve (Normal Distribution)
A symmetrical, bell-shaped curve where mean = mode = median. Radiation counting data, when plotted, approximates a Bell Curve.
Accuracy
How close a measurement is to the true value. Describes systematic errors. Example: 11,995 counts vs. 12,000 true counts is highly accurate.
Precision
The reproducibility or consistency of repeated measurements. A set of counts with low scatter around the mean is precise. Precision increases with more counts.
Poisson Distribution
A statistical model for random discrete events (like radioactive disintegrations). Radioactive decay follows Poisson distribution. Approximates a Bell Curve when plotted.
Gaussian Distribution
Also called the Normal Distribution. Similar to Poisson distribution; used for large numbers of counts. The familiar symmetric Bell Curve shape.
Standard Deviation (σ)
A measure of how tightly data clusters around the mean. For a single count n of radioactive source, σ = √n. 1σ ≈ 68% confidence, 2σ ≈ 95%, 3σ ≈ 99%.
Percent Standard Deviation (%σ)
Expresses statistical error as a percentage: %σ = 100/√n. As count rate increases, %σ decreases — more counts = better precision.
Dead Time
The time required by a gamma camera to process one count before accepting another. At very high count rates, the camera “misses” counts, reducing precision.
Nuclei (N)
The number of radioactive atoms in a radionuclide sample. As nuclei decay (disintegrate), the number decreases over time.
Decay Constant (λ)
The fraction of radioactive nuclei that decay per unit time. λ = 0.693 / T½. Units: per time (hr¹, min¹, etc.).
Decay Rate (DR)
The number of nuclei decaying per unit time. DR = λN = (0.693/T½) × N. Equivalent to the activity of the source.
Decay Factor (DF)
The fraction of original activity remaining after a given time. Post-decay DF = e−λt (always < 1). Pre-decay DF = eλt (always > 1). DF at t=0 = 1.
Post Decay
Calculating the activity remaining after a known elapsed time. Uses the equation A = A0 × e−λt. The post decay factor is always < 1.
Pre Decay
Calculating the activity that existed before time = 0. Uses the equation A0 = A × eλt. The pre decay factor is always > 1.
Decay Equation
A = A0 e−λt for post-decay; A0 = A eλt for pre-decay. Where A0 = initial activity, A = activity at time t, λ = decay constant, t = elapsed time.
Euler’s Number (e)
The mathematical constant ≈ 2.718. The base of the natural logarithm. Used in the decay equation because radioactive decay is an exponential process.
Decay Factor Chart
A reference table providing pre-calculated DFs for various values of t/T½. Steps: (1) calculate t/T½, (2) round to 3 sig figs, (3) find row (first 2 digits) and column (3rd digit).
Specific Concentration
The activity per unit volume of a radiopharmaceutical. Most commonly expressed as mCi/mL. Formula: Conc = Activity / Volume.
Volume Calculation
Finding the volume needed from a known concentration. Formula: Volume = Activity needed / Concentration. Can incorporate decay if concentration is at a different time.
Precalibration Factor
The pre-decay factor applied to each dose when preparing kits for future injections. Accounts for decay between kit preparation time and injection time.
Total Activity (Kit Prep)
The sum of all pre-calibrated individual dose amounts needed for a full day’s injections. Each dose × its precalibration factor = adjusted activity required at preparation time.
