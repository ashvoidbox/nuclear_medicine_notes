What do radionuclides do?

What happens in decay??

  • So we have:

  1. Alpha
  2. Beta
  3. Positron
  4. Isomeric Transition
  5. Electron Capture
  6. Fission

In all decay modes, energy, charge, and mass are conserved

Alpha Decay

Alpha (α) Decay

  • Alpha (α) Decay:
    • Occurs in radionuclides that are neutron deficient
      • The parent has too many protons relative to neutrons
    • Alpha decay occurs by the nucleus emitting an alpha particle (α)
      • An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons
      • Written as 42He or α

What does Alpha Decay do to a radionuclide?

  • Alpha decay occurs by: X → Y + α
    • Element X decays to element Y plus an alpha particle
  • Alpha decay changes:
    • Mass number (A) decreases by 4
    • Atomic number (Z) decreases by 2
  • So: A# decreases by 4, Z# decreases by 2

Properties of Alpha Particles:

  • Largest and heaviest type of radiation
  • Slowest — travels only a few centimeters in air
  • Stopped by a sheet of paper or skin
  • Least penetrating of all radiation types
  • Positively charged (+2)

Beta Decay

Beta Decay

  • Beta (β-) Decay:
  • Occurs in radionuclides that are neutron rich
    • Has a high n : Z ratio
  • Beta decay occurs by: X → Y + β⁻ + antineutrino
    • Element X decays to element Y plus a β- and an antineutrino
  • Beta particles are just electrons from the nucleus
  • They can have a + or - charge
  • A - charge is identical to an Electron
  • A + charge is called a Positron
    • We'll talk about positron's in a little bit
  • Every particle (for example protons and electrons) has an opposite particle, called antimatter
  • Electrons have an antimatter of antineutrino's
  • Antineutrino's function is to carry away excess energy from the reaction

Beta (β-) Decay changes:

  • To get a β- the nucleus converts a neutron into a proton and a β-
    • n → p + β⁻
  • We are losing a neutron but gaining a proton
  • Our Mass (A) # stays the same (losing neutron, gaining proton)
  • Our Atomic (Z) # increases by 1 (gaining a proton)
  • So: A# does not change but Z# increases by 1

Beta (β-) Decay - Examples:

    • Th-234 decayed to Pa-234 plus a beta particle and an antineutrino
    • Mass # stayed the same
    • Atomic # increased by 1

Positron Decay

Positron Decay

  • Positron (β⁺) Decay:
    • Occurs in radionuclides that are proton rich
      • Has a high Z : n ratio
    • β⁺ decay occurs by: X → Y + β⁺ + neutrino
      • Element X decays to element Y plus a β+ and a neutrino

What is a Positron (β⁺)?

  • A positron is still a Beta particle
    • Except now it has a positive charge
    • Therefore it's an electron with a positive charge
  • A radionuclide that decays by positron must have a transition energy of at least 1.02 MeV
  • 1.02 MeV comes from:
    • The mass of a proton and electron are both equal to an energy of about 0.511 MeV
    • The positron given off will have all that energy
  • This is called pair production — converting energy into mass

What happens to the Positron

  • The β⁺ (which is an e+) flies out of the nucleus
  • It will quickly come in contact with an e-
    • Remember unlike charges attract
  • When the 2 (e+ and e-) collide, Annihilation occurs
    • Annihilation destroys the mass
    • However the mass is converted back to its original energy
    • 2 photons in the form of gamma rays (γ) are given off at 180° from each other
    • Each gamma has 0.511 MeV of energy (total = 1.02 MeV)

Positron (β⁺) Decay changes:

  • We have too many protons, so a proton is converted to a neutron
  • p → n + β⁺
  • Mass (A) # does not change
  • Atomic (Z) # decreases by 1
  • So: A# does not change but Z# decreases by 1

Example:

    • Carbon-10 decays by positron to Boron-10
    • A# stays the same, Z# decreases by 1

Electron Capture

Electron Capture

  • Electron Capture (EC) Decay:
    • Occurs in radionuclides that are proton rich
      • Hmmm; Positron decay radionuclides are proton rich as well.
        • This is an alternate decay for proton rich radionuclides
        • These radionuclides usually have energies less than 1.02 MeV

What happens in Electron Capture

  • Remember these radionuclides are proton rich
    • The nucleus wants to get rid of some protons
    • The nucleus captures an e- from one of the atom's shells
    • The e- combines with a p+ to form a neutron
  • EC decay occurs by: p + e⁻ → n + neutrino
    • A proton plus an electron yields a neutron and a neutrino
  • The changing of the A & Z#'s should look similar to Positron
    • Mass (A) # does not change
    • Atomic (Z) # decreases by 1

What Electron Capture looks like with a Radiopharmaceutical

  • The nucleus of Krypton-81 captures an e- to give you Bromine-81
    • Be-7 decays to Li-7 by Electron Capture
6

Characteristic Radiation

Characteristic Radiation

  • After Electron Capture occurs, a vacancy is left in the electron shell
    • The nucleus captured an e- from the K-shell (most likely)
    • This leaves a "hole" in the K-shell
  • An outer electron falls in to fill the vacancy
    • When an electron moves to a lower energy level, it gives off energy
    • This energy is given off as a characteristic x-ray
  • The x-ray is called "characteristic" because its energy is characteristic of the element

Characteristic x-ray properties:

  • The energy of the characteristic x-ray = difference between the two electron shell energies
  • Ex-ray = Eouter shell - Einner shell (vacancy)
  • These x-rays are used diagnostically in nuclear medicine
    • Example: 201Tl decays by EC and emits characteristic x-rays of mercury (Hg) at 69–83 keV

Isomeric Transition

Isomeric Transition

  • Isomeric Transition (IT):
    • A nucleus can exist in a ground state
      • Each of its protons and neutrons in the lowest possible energy level
    • A nucleus can also exist in above ground states
      • The protons and neutrons have various energy levels above ground state
      • These states are called Isomeric States
    • Isomeric states usually last for a short period of time then return to ground state
      • Usually lasts from seconds to minutes
    • An isomeric state that lasts for a longer period of time is called a metastable state
      • denoted by an "m" after the A# (99mTc)
      • Usually on the order of hours or more
        • 99mTc lasts for 6 hours
  • 2 forms of Isomeric Transition:
    1. Gamma (γ) Ray emission
    2. Internal Conversion
8

Gamma Ray Emission

Gamma Ray Emission

  • A common mode of IT is for an upper level energy state to decay to a lower level energy state
    • Remember in EC when an e- falls to take another's place, an x-ray is given off
    • In Gamma Emission when an upper level energy state within the nucleus decays (falls) to a lower energy state, a γ is given off
      • The energy is Excitation Energy
    • γ comes from the nucleus

Energy of Gamma Ray Emission

  • The energy of a γ is the difference between the 2 isomeric states
  • A decay of a 300 keV isomeric state to a lower isomeric state of 160 keV will produce a γ of 140 keV
    • Does that 140 keV γ sound familiar? (99mTc emits a 140 keV gamma!)

Internal Conversion

Internal Conversion

  • This is still a form of Isomeric Transition
  • The Nucleus is in an excited state
    • Has Excitation Energy
  • When the nucleus decays to a lower state the energy is transferred to an orbital electron
    • No γ is produced in the process
    • The energy just strikes an electron
  • The energy usually strikes a K-shell electron

So then, what is given off in internal conversion

  • So, we have energy striking an electron
  • If the Excitation energy equals or exceeds the binding energy the electron is ejected
    • The ejected electron is called the conversion electron
  • What happens if an electron is ejected??
    • Another electron falls to take its place
    • A characteristic x-ray is given off

Auger Process

Internal Conversion: Auger Process

Internal Conversion can undergo Auger process

  • Auger Process:
    • We still have internal conversion happening
      • That means an e- is ejected and an e- falls to take its place
    • However, the excitation energy from the ejected e- strikes another e-
      • If the energy exceeds binding energy the e- can be ejected
    • The ejected electron is now called the Auger Electron

Fission

Fission

  • Can occur in one of 2 processes:
    1. Spontaneous Fission
    2. Man Made fission
  • We will concentrate on Spontaneous Fission since we are talking about Decay

Spontaneous Fission:

  • The process of a heavy nucleus splitting and breaking into:
    • 2 fragments (fission fragments)
    • 2 - 3 neutrons
    • Excess energy (200 - 400 MeV)
  • Probability of Spontaneous Fission
    • Low probability
    • Increases as the Mass number increases
  • Some radionuclides that can decay by spontaneous fission are:
    • U-235 & Cf-254
  • Remember this is spontaneous fission. You will find a more extensive Fission lesson in your radiopharmacy course...

Speed of Photons and Particles

Speed of Photons and Particles

  • Alpha is the slowest particle
  • Beta is a little faster than alpha
  • Gamma and x-rays are the fastest
    • They move at the same speed (speed of light)

Size of Particles

Size of Particles

  • Alpha - Biggest Particle (helium nucleus: 2p + 2n)
  • Beta - Little smaller than the alpha (electron mass)
  • Gamma - Very small - Same size as an X-ray
  • X-ray - Very small - Same size as a gamma

Penetration of Photons and Particles

Penetration of Photons and Particles

  • Photons and Particles can only go so far before they lose their energy and basically disintegrate
  • Their penetration depends on:
    • Their size
      • As particles get Bigger their penetration decreases
    • Their energy
      • As energy increases penetration of particles and photons increases

Penetration of Photons and Particles:

  • Photons and Particles differ in their penetration
    • Photons go farther and have more penetration than particles
      • They are smaller and have virtually no mass
  • Order of penetration (most to least):
    1. Gamma and x-rays penetrate the same and the most
    2. Beta particles are next in penetration
      • They are a particle so they don't travel very far
    3. Alpha Particles are last in penetration
      • They are a big particle so they travel the least

  • Alpha Particles
    • Usually only travel a few mm and can be stopped by skin
  • Beta Particles
    • Travel a few inches but can be stopped by a small piece of Aluminium
  • Gamma and x-rays
    • Can travel a good distance but stopped by lead

Decay Schemes

Decay Schemes

Below you will find a decay scheme:

Decay Schemes convey information about:

  1. Modes of decay
    • How it decays
    • Percent it decays by
    • Energy of the decayed product
  2. Byproducts of decay
  3. Half-Life

Decay Schemes:

  • Modes of Decay
    • Displayed by particle or photon that is given off (α, β, γ, etc...)
    • Arrows depict how the nucleus is changing
      • An arrow slanting to the right means an increase in protons (β- Decay)
      • An arrow slanting to the left means a decrease in protons (β+ Decay & EC)
      • An arrow pointing down means just a change in energy (Isomeric Transition)
    • The % shown is the percent the RN is decaying to that particular particle or photon
    • The energy is the energy of the particular particle or photon given off

Glossary

Alpha Decay
A radioactive decay process in which a nucleus emits an alpha particle (42He), decreasing the atomic number (Z) by 2 and mass number (A) by 4. Occurs in proton-rich, heavy nuclei.
Alpha Particle
A particle consisting of 2 protons and 2 neutrons (helium-4 nucleus). Has a +2 charge. The largest and least penetrating type of radiation; stopped by skin or paper.
Antineutrino
The antimatter counterpart of a neutrino, emitted during beta-minus decay. Carries away excess energy from the reaction.
Annihilation
The process by which a positron (β⁺) collides with an electron, converting their combined mass into two gamma photons, each with 0.511 MeV of energy, emitted at 180° from each other.
Auger Electron
An electron ejected from an atom during the Auger process, when the characteristic x-ray energy released from electron shell transition is absorbed by another orbital electron, ejecting it.
Beta Decay (β⁻)
Radioactive decay in which a neutron-rich nucleus converts a neutron into a proton, emitting a beta-minus particle (electron) and an antineutrino. Z increases by 1, A stays the same.
Beta Particle
A high-speed electron (β⁻) or positron (β⁺) emitted from a nucleus during beta decay. More penetrating than alpha particles; stopped by a few mm of aluminum.
Characteristic X-ray
An x-ray emitted when an outer-shell electron fills a vacancy in an inner shell, releasing energy equal to the difference between the two shell binding energies. Energy is characteristic of the element.
Conversion Electron
An electron ejected from an orbital shell during internal conversion, when excitation energy from the nucleus is transferred directly to the electron instead of being emitted as a gamma ray.
Daughter Radionuclide
The more stable product nucleus resulting from radioactive decay of the parent radionuclide.
Decay Scheme
A diagram showing the decay modes of a radionuclide, including the type of decay, energy of emitted particles/photons, percent abundance, and half-life.
Electron Capture (EC)
A decay process in which a proton-rich nucleus captures an inner orbital electron, converting a proton into a neutron. Z decreases by 1, A stays the same. Produces characteristic x-rays.
Excitation Energy
The energy above ground state that a nucleus possesses when in an isomeric (excited) state. Released as a gamma ray or transferred to an orbital electron during de-excitation.
Fission (Spontaneous)
A nuclear decay process in which a heavy nucleus spontaneously splits into two fission fragments, 2–3 neutrons, and 200–400 MeV of excess energy. Probability increases with mass number.
Ground State
The lowest energy state of a nucleus, in which all protons and neutrons are at the lowest possible energy levels.
Isomeric State
An excited energy state of a nucleus above the ground state. Short-lived isomeric states decay quickly; longer-lived states are called metastable states.
Isomeric Transition (IT)
A decay process in which a nucleus in an excited (metastable) isomeric state releases energy and returns to ground state, either by gamma ray emission or internal conversion.
Internal Conversion
A form of isomeric transition in which excitation energy is transferred from the nucleus directly to an orbital electron (usually K-shell), ejecting it as a conversion electron. No gamma ray is produced.
Metastable State
A long-lived isomeric state denoted by 'm' (e.g., 99mTc). Typically lasts hours or more. 99mTc has a half-life of 6 hours.
Neutrino
A nearly massless, chargeless particle emitted during positron decay and electron capture to carry away excess energy.
Pair Production
The conversion of energy (≥1.02 MeV) into mass in the form of a positron-electron pair. The reverse process (annihilation) converts the pair back into two 0.511 MeV gamma photons.
Parent Radionuclide
The unstable nucleus that undergoes radioactive decay to produce a more stable daughter nucleus.
Positron (β⁺)
An antimatter electron with a positive charge (+1). Emitted during positron decay. Undergoes annihilation with a nearby electron, producing two 0.511 MeV gamma photons.
Positron Decay (β⁺)
Radioactive decay in which a proton-rich nucleus converts a proton into a neutron, emitting a positron (β⁺) and a neutrino. Requires ≥1.02 MeV transition energy. Z decreases by 1, A stays the same.
Radionuclide
An unstable atom that undergoes radioactive decay, emitting radiation to achieve a more stable configuration.
Spontaneous Fission
See Fission (Spontaneous). A natural decay mode of very heavy nuclei.
Tc-99m
99mTc (Technetium-99m) — the most widely used radiopharmaceutical in nuclear medicine. A metastable isomer that decays by isomeric transition, emitting a 140 keV gamma ray with a 6-hour half-life.
Transmutation
The conversion of one element into another through radioactive decay (change in atomic number Z).
