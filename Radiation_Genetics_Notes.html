Introduction

Radiation Genetics deals with:

  • Causes and effects of genetic mutations
  • Effects of radiation on DNA
  • Chromosome aberrations
  • Repair vs. mutation

This unit is part of the RadioBiology course and connects directly to cell biology and radiation interaction concepts covered earlier.

Genetic Mutations

Genetic Mutations

  • A mutation is a change in the structure
  • A genetic mutation is a change in DNA structure
    • In our case, it's a change caused by ionizing radiation
3

DNA and Chromosome Aberrations

What are DNA and Chromosomes?

  • DNA
    • Has 2 strands that twist around each other
    • The twists form a double helix shape
    • Directs cell activity
  • Chromosomes
    • Linear threads in the nucleus of a cell
      • The centromere divides the chromosome into 2 arms
    • They contain the DNA
    • We have 46 (23 pairs) of chromosomes

Types of DNA Strand Breaks from Radiation

DNA has 2 strands. When DNA gets struck with radiation, 2 types of mutations can occur:

  1. Single Strand Breaks — Point Mutations
  2. Double Strand Breaks — Frameshift Mutations
4

DNA Strand Breaks

DNA Single Strand Breaks

Radiation can break a single strand of DNA:

  • A single strand break is usually from low LET radiation:
    • Minor Break
      • It repairs (rejoins) itself
    • Major Break
      • It doesn't repair itself
      • It will get oxidized and not rejoin

DNA Double Strand Breaks

Radiation can break both strands of DNA:

  • A double strand break is usually from high LET radiation:
    • The fragments will be separated and not rejoin
5

DNA Mutations

What happens when DNA is mutated?

  • The parent cell that contains the DNA will eventually divide to replicate itself
    • When the cell divides, information from the mutated DNA will not transfer correctly
    • The new cell will be different from the original
  • The mutation may also lead to cell death
6

Chromosome Aberrations

Chromosome Aberrations

Chromosomes can be irradiated and become aberrated:

  • Single Hit Chromosome Aberrations
    • Caused by low LET radiation
  • Multi-Hit Chromosome Aberrations
    • Caused by high LET radiation, high dose rate, and large doses
  • Acentric and Dicentric Aberrated Fragments
    • An acentric fragment is a segment of a chromosome that lacks a centromere
    • A dicentric chromosome is an abnormal chromosome with two centromeres
7

Single-Hit Aberrations

Single Hit Chromosome Aberrations — 4 Effects

  1. Terminal Deletion
    • A section of the chromosome breaks and is lost
  2. Inversion
    • A section of the chromosome breaks, inverts itself then recombines
    • Information in chromosome is changed
  3. Duplication
    • Piece of the chromosome breaks and reattaches itself to another chromosome
    • Information in chromosome is changed
  4. Recombination
    • Piece of the chromosome breaks and reattaches itself to its original chromosome
8

Multi-Hit Aberrations

Multi-Hit Chromosome Aberrations — 4 Effects

  1. Interstitial Deletion
    • Same as Terminal Deletion except 2 segments are formed
  2. Inversion
    • Same as single break except 2 segments break off, invert, and reattach
  3. Duplication
    • Two segments are produced and one or both segments attach to another chromosome
  4. Translocation
    • Two chromosomes exchange segments
9

Acentric and Dicentric Fragments

Acentric and Dicentric Fragments

Radiation hits can produce acentric and dicentric fragments:

  • Two separate chromosomes are hit by a single radiation event
  • Both chromosomes break and their parts reattach
    • Dicentric Fragment — contains the centromere and an arm (chromatid)
      • Appears as 2 centromeres
    • Acentric Fragment — contains the broken ends of the chromosomes
      • Genetic information is lost
      • The cell is altered or dies

Repair vs. Mutation

Repair vs. Mutation

Once cells are irradiated they will either repair themselves or become permanently mutated.

Repair after irradiation depends on:

  • Sites of break in the DNA or chromosome
  • Total radiation dose
  • Dose Rate
    • High dose rate increases double strand breaks because there is less time to repair
  • LET of the radiation
    • High LET creates more double strand breaks
      • α-particles, protons, and neutrons cause more chromosome aberrations than gamma-rays

Cellular Response to Radiation

Cellular Response to Radiation — Overview

Cells respond to radiation through several mechanisms:

  • Target Theory
  • Direct vs. Indirect Radiation
  • Consequences due to radiation on the cell
  • Survival Curves
    • Response of cells to radiation
12

Target Theory

Target Theory

When radiation interacts with cells it is called a "Hit". The Target Theory explains which hits have serious consequences:

  • Cells have an abundance of some molecules
    • Most of these are organelles
  • Cells do not have an abundance of some molecules
    • Chromosomes which contain the DNA
      • Necessary for cell survival
    • These are called critical (target) molecules
  • A serious consequence is considered cell death

When Does a Hit Have Serious Consequences?

  • Hit on an abundant molecule (organelles)
    • Usually does not result in cell injury
    • Other similar molecules will take over its responsibilities
  • Hit on a Target Molecule (DNA/chromosomes)
    • Results in cell injury and possibly death
      • Cell death occurs when the target molecule is inactivated
    • The cell does not have other molecules to take over its functions
13

Direct Hit vs. Indirect Hit

Direct and Indirect Radiation Both Cause Hits

  • Direct Hit — interacts directly with the critical molecule
    • Targets the DNA itself
    • Occurs more with high LET radiations
  • Indirect Hit — interacts outside the critical molecule
    • Targets organelles or surrounding water (free radicals)
14

Cell Survival Curves

Cell Survival Curves

When cells are irradiated their results differ. Radiation damage depends on:

  • Total Dose
  • Dose Rate
  • LET of the radiation
  • Particular stage of the cell cycle
  • Type of the cell

The cellular response to radiation is illustrated by cell survival curves.

Plotting Cell Survival Curves

  • X-axis: Dose (linear scale)
  • Y-axis: Surviving fraction of cells (logarithmic scale)
    • Surviving cells retain all reproductive and functional activities
  • A basic survival curve shows that as dose increases, the fraction of surviving cells decreases

How Many Hits to Kill a Cell?

  • Radiation is deposited in cells randomly
  • Simple cells: usually 1 direct hit kills the cell → Linear (straight line) curve
  • Complex cells: takes more than one hit → show a shouldered curve
    • Cells are able to repair themselves
    • As dose increases, cells don't have time to repair before more hits occur

Survival Curves by Radiation Type

  • High LET Radiation (α-particles, fast neutrons)
    • Curves are nearly a straight line (linear)
    • Radiation is strong enough to kill cells with a single hit
  • Low LET Radiation (gamma rays, x-rays)
    • Curves show a shoulder before the linear portion
    • Until dose reaches a certain point, some cells repair themselves and are not killed

Glossary

Mutation
A change in the structure of genetic material (DNA).
Genetic Mutation
A change in DNA structure, in this context caused by ionizing radiation.
DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid)
Double-stranded helix molecule that directs all cell activity and carries genetic information.
Chromosome
Linear thread in the nucleus of a cell containing DNA. Humans have 46 (23 pairs).
Centromere
The region that divides a chromosome into two arms; essential for proper cell division.
Single Strand Break
Break in one strand of DNA, usually caused by low LET radiation. May repair itself (minor) or become permanent (major).
Double Strand Break
Break in both strands of DNA, usually from high LET radiation. Fragments separate and do not rejoin.
Point Mutation
A mutation resulting from a single strand break; affects a single base pair.
Frameshift Mutation
A mutation resulting from a double strand break; shifts the reading frame of the genetic code.
Single-Hit Aberration
Chromosome damage caused by a single ionizing event (low LET). Produces terminal deletion, inversion, duplication, or recombination.
Multi-Hit Aberration
Chromosome damage from multiple ionizing events (high LET, high dose rate). Produces interstitial deletion, inversion, duplication, or translocation.
Terminal Deletion
A section of the chromosome breaks off and is permanently lost.
Interstitial Deletion
Like terminal deletion but two segments are formed (multi-hit).
Inversion
A segment breaks, inverts, and recombines — changing the DNA sequence without loss of material.
Duplication
A chromosome segment breaks and reattaches to another chromosome, duplicating genetic information.
Translocation
Two chromosomes exchange segments with each other (multi-hit aberration).
Acentric Fragment
A chromosome segment that lacks a centromere; genetic information is lost and the cell may die.
Dicentric Chromosome
An abnormal chromosome with two centromeres, resulting from radiation-induced rearrangement.
Target Theory
Theory that radiation must hit a critical (target) molecule (DNA/chromosomes) to cause serious cell injury or death.
Critical Molecule
A molecule present in limited supply (e.g., chromosomes) whose inactivation leads to cell death.
Direct Hit
Radiation that interacts directly with the DNA molecule. More common with high LET radiation.
Indirect Hit
Radiation that interacts with surrounding molecules (e.g., water to form free radicals), which then damage DNA.
Cell Survival Curve
A graph of surviving cell fraction (log scale, Y-axis) vs. dose (linear, X-axis) showing how radiation kills cells.
Linear Survival Curve
Straight-line survival curve seen with high LET radiation; single hits kill cells directly.
Shouldered Survival Curve
Curve with an initial flat "shoulder" region seen with low LET radiation; cells can repair sub-lethal damage until dose overwhelms repair capacity.
LET (Linear Energy Transfer)
The amount of energy deposited per unit length of path by ionizing radiation. High LET = more DNA damage.
